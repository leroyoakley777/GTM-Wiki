# Buyer's Journey - the buyer's view of the sales process

This page is the buyer's view of a deal. The [sales process: the MEDDPICC
flow](../flows/sales-process-meddpicc) is the seller's view: six stages a rep
runs, each gated by exit criteria. The buyer's journey is the same arc seen
from the other side of the table. The seller is at Proposal while the buyer is
still in Evaluation; the seller pushes Discovery when the buyer only has a
latent problem. A deal stalls or dies when the two arcs fall out of step.

- **Read the seller's view first.** This page assumes the six MEDDPICC stages
  and the value hypothesis artifact. Everything here is the mirror of that
  flow.
- **The buyer is the source of the exit criteria.** The seller's stage gate is
  a proxy for where the buyer actually is. The buyer's journey teaches you to
  read the buyer's position directly, so the gate matches reality and not the
  CRM.
- **Research on B2B buying consistently finds the buyer completes most of the
  journey before a vendor is ever contacted.** 6sense's 2025 Buyer Experience
  Report (about 4,000 buyers) puts first contact at 61% of the journey, down
  from 69% in 2024, and Gartner's Future of Sales research (2020) finds buyers
  spend only about 17% of their buying time with suppliers. Every figure on
  this page is named and dated in [the sources section](#every-figure-named-and-dated).
  The operational consequence is not a number: it is that a seller who
  qualifies only from their own pitch is qualifying against a ghost.

The [Account Executive role](../roles/aes/) owns this motion. The buyer's
journey also differs by channel, so each stage below cross-links to the channel
page that changes who supplies proof at that point.

---

## Six positions at a glance

The seller's six MEDDPICC stages and the buyer's six positions are the same
deal seen from two sides.

| # | Buyer's position | What the buyer does | Seller's MEDDPICC stage | Letters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Active problem | Realizes the problem, builds urgency | Qualification | M, E |
| 2 | Solution discovery | Researches approaches and vendors, forms a shortlist | Discovery | D, D |
| 3 | Evaluation | Compares vendors on criteria, recruits a champion | Scoping | E, M |
| 4 | Decision | Makes the pick, mobilizes the economic buyer | Evaluating | P, C |
| 5 | Negotiation and purchase | Defends budget through procurement and legal | Proposal and Negotiation | C |
| 6 | Adoption to expansion | Implements, realizes value, renews | Closed-Won and post-sale | C (compression) |

Each position below carries the same contract the seller's page does: what the
buyer is doing, what the seller must supply to meet them there, the in-channel
angle, the resource-versus-artifact line, the exit criteria the buyer must have
met, and the buyer signals that tell you the arcs have drifted.

---

## 1. Active problem (Qualification)

**Job (buyer):** decide the problem is real, worth fixing, and worth your time
to fix now. This is where a problem becomes a priority, or quietly does not.

**Activities**

- The buyer names the problem and how it is hurting a metric they own.
- They build a rough sense of what it costs to leave it alone.
- They start to decide whether this is a project or a distraction.
- They may not have a budget line yet, but they can see one forming.

**What the seller must supply**

- Read the account for a real problem, not a manufactured one. The buyer
  already knows whether they are hurting; the seller's job at this position is
  to reflect that back accurately, not to create urgency the buyer does not
  feel.
- Qualify against the account as the buyer sees it. If they do not own the
  metric the product moves, there is no deal here yet, only a conversation.
- Confirm who the economic buyer is and whether the problem is felt above
  them.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- A partner who owns the account often sees budget and pain a vendor cannot.
  Their read at this position is the buyer's true temperature, not the
  seller's pitch.
- File deal registration early so the partner's margin is locked and no second
  vendor farms the same account.

**Resources vs artifacts**

- A resource the seller uses: a [territory plan](../flows/sales-process-meddpicc)
  and an ICP definition to qualify against.
- An artifact the deal produces: a first-pass value hypothesis, marked as a
  guess, with the buyer's own statement of the problem quoted.

**Exit criteria (what the buyer must have shown)**

- [ ] The buyer names a problem with a business impact in their terms.
- [ ] A metric the buyer owns is affected.
- [ ] A path to the economic buyer exists, or the buyer can name who controls
      access.
- [ ] A next step with a date and an owner.

**Buyer signals of misalignment**

- The seller is building urgency while the buyer keeps rescheduling. The buyer
  is not at Active problem yet, or the problem is not a priority.
- The buyer talks about the pitch while the seller talks about the problem.

## 2. Solution discovery (Discovery)

**Job (buyer):** learn how the problem is solved, form a shortlist, and
understand what good looks like before anyone is evaluated.

**Activities**

- The buyer researches approaches: build, buy, change process, or do nothing.
- They look for evidence of results from teams like theirs.
- They form an initial shortlist of vendors, often without a vendor knowing it.
- They start to surface decision criteria, even if informally.

**What the seller must supply**

- A discovery conversation that finds the buyer's numbers, not the seller's.
  The value hypothesis is written with the buyer's stated cost and an as-of
  date, never with a number the seller supplied.
- Help the buyer name their decision criteria in their words. A buyer with
  fuzzy criteria evaluates on price and vibes, and both are bad.
- Name the competition the buyer is actually considering, including the
  do-nothing option.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner opens doors to stakeholders the seller cannot reach and validates
  the problem in terms the buyer's team trusts.
- A partner who cannot articulate the problem is not engaged. The buyer's
  shortlist is forming with or without this seller, and a disengaged partner
  quietly drops this vendor from it.

**Resources vs artifacts**

- A resource: a discovery question bank and a first-meeting deck skeleton.
- An artifact: the value hypothesis updated with the buyer's own numbers and
  the buyer's named decision criteria.

**Exit criteria**

- [ ] The buyer has stated the cost of the problem with a number and an as-of
      date.
- [ ] Decision criteria are named in the buyer's words.
- [ ] Competition is named, including do-nothing.
- [ ] A champion is identified or emerging on the buyer's side.

**Buyer signals of misalignment**

- The seller is delivering product education while the buyer is still
  self-educating, or the buyer has quietly gone dark and is comparing options
  elsewhere.

## 3. Evaluation (Scoping)

**Job (buyer):** decide what good looks like, recruit an internal sponsor, and
shape the decision so it can actually happen.

**Activities**

- The buyer defines scope: who is in, what success looks like, and the
  timeline.
- They map the decision process and who gets a vote.
- They recruit or confirm a champion who will advocate internally.
- They size the value in their own units and start the paper process they know
  is coming.

**What the seller must supply**

- A scoping document that mirrors the buyer's process, stakeholders, and value,
  not the seller's product structure.
- A named value model so the economics are worked, not vibed, in the buyer's
  units.
- Start procurement early with the buyer, not at proposal. A security review
  the buyer knows about at scoping is a schedule; one discovered at signature
  is a killer.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner sizes implementation and services so scope is accurate before
  pricing.
- For VAR-led deals the partner owns scope; the seller verifies the buyer's
  value and economic-buyer facts themselves.

**Resources vs artifacts**

- A resource: a scoping checklist and a value model.
- An artifact: a scoping document that names the buyer's stakeholders, process,
  and value, and becomes the map the evaluation runs against.

**Exit criteria**

- [ ] The buyer has a decision process with named people and steps.
- [ ] Paper-process requirements are known and scheduled.
- [ ] Value is sized in the buyer's units with a named base.
- [ ] Scope is agreed in writing.

**Buyer signals of misalignment**

- The seller is pushing for the pick while the buyer has not yet recruited an
  internal sponsor. Evaluation without a champion is a survey, not a decision.

---

## 4. Decision (Evaluating)

**Job (buyer):** make the pick, defend it, and mobilize the economic buyer.

**Activities**

- The buyer runs the evaluation: pilot, demo, reference calls, scored against
  the named criteria.
- The champion builds the internal case and defends the recommendation.
- The economic buyer is brought in and aligned on value, not just price.
- The buyer narrows to a shortlist and then to a pick.

**What the seller must supply**

- Direct answers to the written criteria, every one, with evidence. An
  unanswered criterion is a reason the buyer cannot defend the pick.
- Ammunition the champion can use internally: ROI, comparisons, success
  stories.
- Awareness of every stakeholder's stance, not just the champion's. The deal is
  won when the committee can say yes, not when one person can.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner champions the seller internally where the customer trusts the
  partner more than a vendor rep.
- The partner's rep joins evaluation sessions as a second voice.

**Resources vs artifacts**

- A resource: a comparison matrix and a champion enablement pack.
- An artifact: an evaluation status document with every stakeholder, their
  stance, and their open concern, updated after every touchpoint.

**Exit criteria**

- [ ] A named champion is actively selling with internal credibility.
- [ ] Every decision-criteria item has a written response.
- [ ] The economic buyer is engaged and aligned on value.
- [ ] Procurement is engaged, not surprised.

**Buyer signals of misalignment**

- The seller believes the deal is at Decision while the committee is still
  comparing against the incumbent. A seller who only tracks the champion is
  reading one voter.

## 5. Negotiation and purchase (Proposal and Negotiation)

**Job (buyer):** defend the budget through procurement and legal, and get the
deal signed.

**Activities**

- The buyer maps the proposal to the decision criteria and checks nothing is
  missing.
- Procurement negotiates price; the economic buyer negotiates value. The two
  are different negotiations.
- The buyer runs the paper process to signature on a named timeline.
- The champion defends value when procurement pushes on price.

**What the seller must supply**

- A proposal that maps line for line to the decision criteria. A missing
  criterion is a negotiation lever for the buyer, not a detail.
- Negotiate with the economic buyer on value, not just with procurement on
  price. Lose the value negotiation and the price negotiation is pure
  discounting.
- Keep the paper process moving so the close is not blocked on the buyer's own
  legal queue.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner negotiates margin and co-terms. Deal registration protects the
  price, and the partner defends it because their margin depends on it.
- For VARs, align on who concedes what before the meeting so margin does not
  evaporate in the room.

**Resources vs artifacts**

- A resource: pricing and negotiation guidelines with a discount authority
  table, and a negotiation plan.
- An artifact: the proposal plus a negotiation plan recording every concession
  and its margin cost.

**Exit criteria**

- [ ] The economic buyer is aligned on value, not just price.
- [ ] The paper process is complete or on a named timeline.
- [ ] Every open objection has a written response.
- [ ] A signature date is agreed with the buyer.

**Buyer signals of misalignment**

- The seller thinks the buyer has decided while the buyer is still running a
  silent vendor-consolidation review. The signature date slips and nobody can
  say why.

## 6. Adoption to expansion (Closed-Won and post-sale)

**Job (buyer):** realize the value, prove it internally, and renew or expand.

**Activities**

- The buyer implements and measures against the success metrics.
- They prove the value to leadership, which is what makes renewal defensible.
- They look for the next metric to improve, which is the expansion opening.

**What the seller must supply**

- A written handoff that carries the value hypothesis metrics forward, so
  success is measured against what was promised.
- Preserve the champion. The champion is the cheapest expansion motion the
  seller has.
- Document the expansion path: the next metric and the next buyer.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner executes delivery and implementation where they own it, and is in
  the handoff so the account relationship survives the sales-to-delivery
  transition.

**Resources vs artifacts**

- A resource: a transition checklist and a kickoff template.
- An artifact: a signed deal plus a handoff document with what was promised,
  the success metrics, the stakeholders, and the next expansion step.

**Exit criteria**

- [ ] Contract executed and PO received.
- [ ] Implementation owner named.
- [ ] Value hypothesis metrics carried into kickoff.
- [ ] An expansion path documented.

**Buyer signals of misalignment**

- The seller counts the account closed while the buyer has not realized value
  yet. A renewal debated on price in a year is the price of closing the value
  loop too early.

## A worked deal, end to end

Acme, a mid-market marketing ops team, $50k ACV. The buyer is the VP of
Operations. The [sales-process page](../flows/sales-process-meddpicc) walks
this deal from the seller's side; this is the same deal from the buyer's.

**Position 1 - Active problem.** The VP realizes revenue reporting is wrong and
the team rebuilds it manually every month. It is a real problem, felt weekly,
but it has not yet become a project. It qualifies on the buyer's side when the
VP starts to see the cost as a line item.

**Position 2 - Solution discovery.** The VP researches: build a tool in-house,
keep the consultant, or buy. The team estimates the manual rebuild costs about
two hours a day. Two hours times 250 working days times $60 an hour of burden
is about $30k a year of wasted time. That is the buyer's number, on the buyer's
base. Decision criteria form: accuracy, time to report, and cost.

**Position 3 - Evaluation.** The buyer maps the decision: the RevOps lead
evaluates, the VP approves, Finance signs. The RevOps lead becomes the
champion. A security review that takes three weeks is put on the calendar now,
not discovered at signature.

**Position 4 - Decision.** A pilot runs on one report, scored against the three
criteria. The buyer compares the seller against the incumbent consultant. The
pilot beats the consultant on timeline, which is the differentiator the
champion uses to defend the pick. The VP, the economic buyer, is aligned on the
$30k-a-year value, not just the price.

**Position 5 - Negotiation and purchase.** The proposal maps to the three
criteria. List is $50k. Procurement asks for 20% off. The seller holds price and
adds a $3k services credit, which costs less margin than the 20% discount. The
VP defends the value through procurement, and the security review completes on
its named timeline.

**Position 6 - Adoption to expansion.** Signed at $50k, PO issued. The handoff
carries the $30k-a-year and accuracy metrics forward. The expansion path is the
next report the team wants fixed, which is the next metric the buyer owns.

**The arithmetic you should trust.** At a 21% Opp to Closed-Won rate, one win
needs about five qualified opportunities. Five qualified opps times a 21% close
rate is just over one win. The 21% figure is Ebsta/Pavilion (2021-2024).
SalesMotion (2026) puts qualified win rates near 29%, which still means three to
four qualified opps per win. The same base governs the seller's page: hold four
qualified opps and expect to lose one. None of this arithmetic changes because
the view is the buyer's; the buyer's journey tells you which of those opps are
real, because a deal at the wrong buyer position is not a qualified deal at all.

## Every figure, named and dated

Every figure on this page comes from a vetted, primary source in the wiki's
source registry (`RESEARCH/gtm-wiki/SOURCES_REGISTRY.md`). No invented stat.

| Claim on this page | Figure | Source (registry) |
|---|---|---|
| The buyer does most of the journey before a seller engages | First contact at 61% of the journey in 2025, down from 69% in 2024; buyers do about two-thirds of the journey before engaging sellers | 6sense, B2B Buyer Experience Report (2025), ~4,000 buyers |
| Buyers spend little time with suppliers | About 17% of total buying time with suppliers | Gartner, Future of Sales research (2020) |
| Buyers prefer a seller-light experience | 61% prefer a rep-free experience (2025); 33% overall and 44% of millennials (2020) | Gartner Sales Survey (2025); Gartner, Future of Sales (2020) |
| The buying decision is large and cross-functional | ~13 people in the decision; 89% of purchases span two or more departments | Forrester, The State of Business Buying (2024) |
| Deals stall when the two arcs fall out of step | 86% of B2B purchases stall; 81% of buyers dissatisfied with their chosen provider | Forrester, The State of Business Buying (2024) |
| The pick is often made before the seller enters | Winning vendor already on the Day One shortlist 95% of the time; ~4 of 5 deals won by the pre-contact favorite | 6sense, B2B Buyer Experience Report (2025) |
| Buyer-side roles the seller must read | Mobilizers, Talkers, Blockers; sellers targeting Mobilizers are 31% more likely to be high performers | CEB, The Challenger Customer (2015) |
| Buyers contact sellers on their own terms | Buyer-initiated contact more than 80% of the time | 6sense, B2B Buyer Experience Report (2025) |

Two of the numbers come from conversion-rate benchmarks rather than buyer
research. The 21% Opp to Closed-Won rate is Ebsta/Pavilion (2021-2024), and the
~29% qualified win rate is SalesMotion (2026). Both are the same bases the
sales-process page uses, so the buyer's view does not invent a second set of
numbers.

The two most commonly repeated B2B buying stats do not ship here because they
do not survive vetting. The old 57% figure from CEB and Google (2012) has been
superseded by Gartner and 6sense's newer numbers, and the claim that buyers
complete 70-80% of the journey before sales contact, often attributed to
Forrester (2024), is a secondary read, not in Forrester's own State of Business
Buying release. Doubtful stays out.

## Failure modes and how to fix them

- **Qualifying against a ghost.** The seller runs the stage gate while the
  buyer has not started the journey. Fix: read the buyer's position directly.
  If the buyer has no champion and no decision process, the deal is not at
  Decision no matter what the CRM says.
- **Ghosted discovery.** The buyer went dark because they moved to a different
  vendor or deferred the project. Fix: keep the buyer's numbers and criteria
  current, and treat silence as a position, not a pause.
- **Champion loss.** The internal sponsor leaves or loses influence. The seller
  finds out at Decision. Fix: track every stakeholder's stance, not just the
  champion, and keep the economic buyer engaged early.
- **Silent procurement.** The buyer is in Negotiation while the seller is still
  in Evaluation. Fix: start the paper process in scoping, with the buyer, so
  procurement is never a surprise.
- **Vendor consolidation.** The buyer runs a quiet shortlist review the seller
  does not see. Fix: the evaluation status document is kept current on every
  touchpoint, so a new competitor appears in the notes, not at signature.
- **Channel misread.** The seller pushes Discovery on a partner-owned account
  that is already at Evaluation. Fix: know who owns the buyer relationship per
  stage (the channel variant below) before pushing your own stage.

## How the journey differs by segment and maturity

- **By deal size.** Under $20k ACV, the buyer's arc is short and often
  self-serve: positions 3 and 4 compress into one quick evaluation and the
  paper process is light. At $250k and up, expect a buying committee of six to
  ten people, positions running in parallel across them, and champions at
  multiple levels. The out-of-step failure is then *within* the committee,
  where one member is at Decision while another is still at Active problem.
- **By segment.** SMB buyers are often product-led and self-serve: they decide
  through the product before sales appears, so the seller enters at position 5
  or 6 and must not reset the arc. Mid-market runs the classic arc. Enterprise
  adds committee selling, where the champion must win multiple evaluators and
  the seller must court every voter, not one.
- **By maturity.** A buyer early in a category is still building the problem
  and needs education and proof at positions 2 and 3. A buyer in a mature
  category knows the problem and the vendors; they compress straight to
  evaluation. The seller's stage gate must move at the buyer's maturity, not
  the seller's calendar.
- **By motion.** The buyer's arc also changes by how they arrived, which is the
  channel variant below.

## Journey by channel

The six positions are the same arc in every channel, but which positions
happen inside the seller's control and who supplies proof at each depends on
how the buyer arrived. Read the channel page for the motion that brought this
buyer in.

| Channel | Where the seller enters | Who supplies proof |
|---|---|---|
| [Inbound](../channels/inbound) | Position 4/5, buyer is largely done | Content and social proof, not the seller; you get one late shot |
| [Outbound](../channels/outbound) | Position 1, often before the buyer owns the problem | Seller builds urgency; your urgency is not theirs |
| [Product-led](../channels/product-led) | Position 2/3, the product is the evaluation | The product and in-app proof; sales enters at Decision |
| [Partner](../channels/partner) | Two-touch, the partner owns the relationship | Partner and vendor co-supply; misread who owns the stage and you push too early |
| [ABM](../channels/abm) | Position 1, seller maps the committee up front | Seller orchestrates; the journey is per-person inside the account |
| [Community-led](../channels/community-led) | Position 2/3, peer proof validates first | Community and members; enthusiasm is not committed budget |
| [Events and field](../channels/events-field) | Any, in-person compresses the arc | The face-to-face seller; a great meeting can manufacture false readiness |
| [Referral / viral](../channels/referral-viral) | Position 3/4, a trusted referrer already validated | The referrer; the buyer may still be comparing against the referrer's other option |

The segment variant says how many buyers and how long the arc. The channel
variant says which positions the seller actually owns and who proves value at
each. A page that teaches the journey without the channel dimension teaches it
wrong.

## Agentic layer

An agent can run the two most mechanical parts of this motion: reading the
CRM to place the buyer at a position, and flagging when the seller's stage and
the buyer's position fall out of step. Both keep a deal from being worked at
the wrong stage.

**Prompt: place the buyer from the deal history.**

```
You are a buyer-journey operator. Given a deal's CRM history (touchpoints,
notes, emails, opportunity stage) and the sales process, return the buyer's
most likely position from these six: Active problem, Solution discovery,
Evaluation, Decision, Negotiation and purchase, Adoption to expansion.
For each position evidence line, quote the touchpoint that supports it (an
email, a note, a stage change). If the evidence is ambiguous between two
positions, return both with a confidence split and the one next action that
would disambiguate. Never infer a position from no evidence: mark it
UNKNOWN. Then return the ONE artifact the seller should ship next for the
buyer's position (value hypothesis update, scoping document, evaluation
status, proposal, handoff).
```

**SOP: flag an out-of-step deal.**

```
For the current deal, load the seller's stage and the buyer's position from
the CRM. If the buyer's position is more than one step behind the seller's
stage, emit a MISALIGNED notice naming both, the evidence for each, and the
recovery move (pull the seller back to the buyer's position, or supply the
artifact that advances the buyer). If the buyer's position is ahead of the
seller's stage, emit a RUSHED notice: the seller is late to the buyer's
readiness. If they match, emit ALIGNED and do nothing else. Never change the
CRM stage on a flag; only report it.
```

This is the same discipline as the rest of the wiki: the flow gates the deal,
the agent reads the buyer's position , and a deal only advances when
the buyer's journey and the seller's stage are actually in step. A deal that
is being worked at the wrong position becomes visible in the notes, not a
surprise at proposal.
