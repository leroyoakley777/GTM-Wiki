# Sales Process - the MEDDPICC Flow

This page is the executable view of a sales process. Six stages, run in
order, each gated by exit criteria. MEDDPICC is the scoring engine: it tells
you what is proven and what is still missing on a deal at any moment.

- **Do not advance on a miss.** Each stage has a hard exit gate. A deal that
  cannot meet it stays in the stage, because skipping a gate makes the next
  stage more expensive, not faster. The stall you see at proposal almost
  always started with a qualification you let slide.
- A resource is not an artifact. A resource is what the seller uses to do
  the work (a territory plan template, a grid, a deck skeleton). An artifact
  is the deliverable the stage produces for a specific deal (a value
  hypothesis, a scoping document, a proposal). The distinction is
  load-bearing: resources make the seller effective, and artifacts move the
  deal. Confuse the two and you will ship templates and call it progress.

Read the companion pages first. The [Account Executive role](../roles/aes/)
owns this motion day to day. [Revenue Architecture](../foundations/revenue-architecture)
is the factory this pipeline feeds. [Outbound](../flows/outbound) is the flow
that feeds this one when a deal starts cold. Then read the [Buyer's
Journey](../flows/buyers-journey), which is this same arc from the buyer's
side: a deal dies when the two views fall out of step.

## MEDDPICC at a glance

Eight letters, eight things to prove on every deal. The flow below tells you
which letters each stage is responsible for.

| Letter | What it means | When it must be proven |
|---|---|---|
| **M** Metrics | The quantified business impact the buyer expects | Discovery |
| **E** Economic Buyer | The person with budget authority who can say yes | Qualification, re-confirmed at negotiation |
| **D** Decision Criteria | The explicit criteria the buyer will judge options against | Discovery |
| **D** Decision Process | The steps and people in the decision | Scoping |
| **P** Paper Process | Procurement, legal, security, and paperwork | Start in scoping, finish at negotiation |
| **I** Identify Pain | The concrete problem, its cause, and its cost | Qualification, deepened in discovery |
| **C** Champion | An internal person selling on your behalf | Evaluating |
| **C** Competition | The status quo and alternatives you are measured against | Discovery, handled in evaluation |

A deal is closed when all eight letters are proven. The flow is the order in
which you prove them.

## Motion at a glance

Six stages, each with a job, a MEDDPICC focus, and an exit gate.

| # | Stage | MEDDPICC focus | Exit gate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Qualification | M, E, I, C-comp, D-criteria | Pain + budget + a path to the economic buyer |
| 2 | Discovery | I, M, C-comp, D-criteria | Pain quantified in the buyer's numbers; criteria and competition named |
| 3 | Scoping | D-criteria, D-process, P, M | Decision process mapped; paper process started; value sized |
| 4 | Evaluating | C-champion, D-process, C-comp, P | A named champion; every criterion answered |
| 5 | Proposal & Negotiation | E, D-process, P, C-comp | Economic buyer aligned; paper process done; a signature date |
| 6 | Closed-Won | E, P, C-champion | Contract executed; handoff documented; expansion path written |

The rest of this page walks each stage, then ships the templates, a worked
deal, the failure modes, the segment variants, and the agentic layer.

## 1. Qualification

**Job:** decide whether the deal is winnable and worth your time. Qualify out
early. A no is a result. A maybe that wastes six weeks is not.

**Activities**

- Confirm the account fits the ICP (buyer, firmographics, a concrete reason
  to reach them). Read [ICP definition](../foundations/icp-definition) before
  you run this stage.
- Get the pain at a high level: what hurts, who else feels it, how long.
- Identify the economic buyer, or confirm a clear path to them.
- Surface what they already evaluated and the status quo they would keep.
- Set a next step with a date, an owner, and a purpose.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner co-qualifies accounts they own. They see budget and pain you
  cannot. Use their read, do not replace yours with it.
- File deal registration early. It locks the partner margin and stops a
  second vendor from farming the same account under a better price.
- Do not qualify alone on a partner account. The partner's relationship is
  the access. Verify the MEDDPICC facts yourself.

**Resources sellers need**

- A territory plan (template in the resources section below).
- The PG 5x5 grid (below) to keep the top five accounts moving.
- The ICP definition, so qualification has a concrete target.

**Artifact this stage produces**

- A first-pass Value Hypothesis (template below): the pain, a rough value,
  the economic buyer, the status quo. It is a guess at this point. Mark it
  as one.

**The real gate (how the operating motion runs it).** Qualification is the
one stage that runs on a light, schedule-shaped gate before any MEDDPICC
letters are proven: a first meeting completed, pain identified, project
timing inside the next six months, and next steps calendared within two
weeks. MEDDPICC is not yet filled in here; it becomes the scoring engine
from Discovery onward. Activities are territory planning, account research
with a value hypothesis as the lens, pipeline-and-prospecting planning, and
initial discovery meetings that schedule the next step.

**Exit criteria (hard gate)**

- [ ] A first meeting has been completed.
- [ ] A pain is identified with a business impact, in the buyer's terms.
- [ ] Project timing is inside the next six months.
- [ ] Next steps are calendared within two weeks, with an owner.
- [ ] A named economic buyer, or a named person who controls access to them,
      or budget exists / the budget process is known.
- [ ] If there is no champion by end of qualification, the account is a maybe,
  not a lead. It stays in the stage.

## 2. Discovery

**Job:** turn the qualification guess into a written value hypothesis with the
buyer's numbers, not yours.

**Activities**

- Ask about the problem, its cause, and what they have already tried. Let
  them talk. You are here to understand, not to pitch.
- Quantify M: current state versus target, in their units. Get the number
  from them, with an as-of date.
- Name the decision criteria and anything they already evaluated.
- Map the competition: the status quo, the do-nothing option, and any
  alternative vendors.
- Confirm the economic buyer and who else affected by the problem.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner joins the discovery call. Their presence opens doors and their
  validation carries weight the vendor voice does not.
- Ask the partner to confirm the pain in their own terms. A partner who
  cannot articulate the problem is a partner who is not engaged.
- Use the partner to reach stakeholders you cannot: ops, finance, IT.

**Resources sellers need**

- A discovery question bank (the strongest questions, organized by MEDDPICC
  letter).
- A first-meeting deck skeleton (template below) to keep the call on rails.

**Artifact this stage produces**

- The Value Hypothesis, updated with the buyer's own numbers and a
  documented as-of date. This becomes the internal contract the rest of the
  deal is measured against.

**The real gate.** Discovery is where the I and C letters first have to
prove themselves. Pain is documented from multiple perspectives (multiple
personas, each with a why), the account is mapped against current tools to
disqualify it if there is no technical fit, business and technical
champions are identified and tested, decision criteria are shaped, and the
team aligns internally on which products are being sold. The stage closes
with a New Business Meeting (NBM) attended by all relevant stakeholders,
where the RD confirms the path to revenue and the SA confirms technical
fit, and the opportunity is updated in the CRM.

**Exit criteria (hard gate)**

- [ ] Pain stated with a number the buyer gave, with an as-of date.
- [ ] Pain documented from multiple perspectives, each persona with a why.
- [ ] Decision criteria named, in their words.
- [ ] Competition named, including do-nothing.
- [ ] A champion is identified or actively emerging.
- [ ] A New Business Meeting (NBM) was held with all relevant stakeholders;
      the RD confirmed the path to revenue and the SA confirmed technical fit.
- [ ] The opportunity is updated in the CRM.

## 3. Scoping

**Job:** define the shape of the deal and the mechanics of the decision before
anything is proposed.

**Activities**

- Define scope: who is in, what the solution touches, what success looks
  like, and the timeline.
- Map the decision process: who evaluates, who approves, in what order.
- Size the value and economics in the buyer's units.
- Start the paper process now: security, legal, procurement, and compliance
  questionnaires. Discovering this at proposal is how deals die in legal.
- Agree the evaluation criteria with the buyer in writing.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner sizes implementation and professional services, so scope is
  accurate before pricing.
- The partner confirms delivery ownership and who bills what. Margin
  conflicts surfaced here are resolvable. Surfaced at signature, they are not.
- For VAR-led deals, the partner owns scope. You verify it.

**Resources sellers need**

- A scoping checklist (who is in, what it touches, timeline, value).
- A value model so the economics are worked, not vibed.
- Architecture diagram instructions and template, a test-criteria library, a
  technical-requirement library, and the POC process / POV map.

**The real gate.** Scoping is where the E letter and both C letters (champion
and competition) start to prove themselves for the activities. Deal size is
updated to whatever the products require, GRC (governance, risk, compliance)
is completed, and a Mutual Action Plan (MAP) is started. A POV (Proof of
Value) approval form is submitted with technical and business context; the
Solution Architect introduces the POV process and builds the POV plan,
including the architecture diagram. Test criteria are developed to match the
business outcomes, the three Ys are updated, and the executive buyer is met
pre-POV.

**Artifacts this stage produces**

- A scoping document / opportunity plan: scope, timeline, value, named
  stakeholders, and the decision process.
- A Mutual Action Plan (MAP).
- A POV test plan and its use cases.
- An executive-buyer go/no-go deck.

**Exit criteria (hard gate)**

- [ ] Deal size is updated to match the products being sold.
- [ ] The Mutual Action Plan (MAP) is drafted and shared.
- [ ] The POV plan is documented (architecture diagram, test criteria).
- [ ] The customer's resources are confirmed available for the POV.
- [ ] The executive buyer has committed to proceed.

## 4. Evaluating

**Job:** win the evaluation and convert the buyer's support into a named
champion who sells for you internally.

**Activities**

- Develop the champion. Give them internal ammunition: ROI, comparisons,
  success stories they can use to defend the recommendation.
- Run the evaluation: pilot, demo, reference calls, and direct answers to the
  written criteria.
- Manage the decision process. Know where you stand with every stakeholder,
  not just your champion.
- Handle the competition in the formal evaluation. A named competitor is an
  addressable problem. An unnamed one is not.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner champions you internally where the customer trusts them more
  than a vendor rep.
- The partner's rep joins evaluation sessions as a second voice and a second
  set of eyes on the room.
- The partner manages the procurement relationships they already hold.

**Resources sellers need**

- A comparison matrix (your product against the named competition on the
  decision criteria).
- A pilot plan: what success looks like, measured, in a bounded timeframe.
- A champion enablement pack: one-pagers and ROI they can forward.

**Artifact this stage produces**

- An evaluation status document: every stakeholder, their stance, their
  score, and their open concern. Update it after every evaluation touchpoint.
  This is the input the proposal stage is built from.

**The real gate.** The POV kickoff call is run, a tight-scoped POV is
delivered that achieves a technical win by solving a prioritized pain, and
an executive-buyer readout is completed. The proposal folds in ROI and a
post-sales plan. Decision process and funding are confirmed, the MAP is
updated, the three Ys are updated, and the champion and executive buyer are
committed to the close path.

**Exit criteria (hard gate)**

- [ ] A POV readout was completed with the executive buyer.
- [ ] A technical win is confirmed (the prioritized pain is solved).
- [ ] Contracting and implementation timelines are aligned.
- [ ] The buying process, funding, and stakeholders are confirmed.
- [ ] The champion and executive buyer are committed to the close path.

## 5. Proposal and Negotiation

**Job:** put the decision criteria into a proposal and negotiate with the
economic buyer, not just procurement.

**Activities**

- Write the proposal that maps line for line to the decision criteria. If a
  criterion is missing, the proposal is not done.
- Negotiate with the economic buyer. Procurement negotiates price. The
  economic buyer negotiates value. Lose the second and the first is just
  discounting.
- Close the paper process: contract, legal, security review, and signatures,
  on a named timeline.
- Use the champion to defend value when procurement pushes on price.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner negotiates margin and co-terms. Deal registration protects your
  price. The partner defends it because their margin depends on it.
- For VARs, align on who concedes what before the meeting. A vendor and VAR
  conceding different things in the same room is how margin evaporates.
- The partner handles the paperwork they own (their contracts, their
  invoicing) so the close is not blocked on their process.

**Resources sellers need**

- Pricing and negotiation guidelines, including a discount authority table.
- A proposal template that mirrors the decision criteria.
- A negotiation plan: what you will concede, what you will not, and what each
  concession costs in margin.

**Artifact this stage produces**

- The proposal, plus a negotiation plan that records every concession and its
  margin cost, so leadership can see what the deal cost to win.

**The real gate.** All MEDDPICC letters are filled out before this stage
closes. Deployment scope and timeline are added to the MAP, the three Ys are
updated, procurement relationships are established, the booking mechanism is
confirmed through the partner where applicable, contract execution is
coordinated, and customer success / post-sales support is introduced.

**Resources sellers need**

- Pricing and negotiation guidelines, including a discount authority table.
- A proposal template that mirrors the decision criteria.
- A negotiation plan: what you will concede, what you will not, and what each
  concession costs in margin.
- The MSA, the contract tooling (such as Ironclad), and the order form.

**Artifacts this stage produces**

- The proposal, plus a negotiation plan recording every concession and its
  margin cost.
- An implementation / adoption framework.

**Exit criteria (hard gate)**

- [ ] Procurement is engaged on the MSA and order forms.
- [ ] The contract is executed (and archived).
- [ ] All MEDDPICC letters are filled and evidenced.

## 6. Closed-Won

**Job:** turn the signed deal into a delivered, expanding account.

**Activities**

- Confirm the economic buyer signed and the paper process is complete (PO
  issued, contract executed).
- Hand off to customer success with a written transition that carries the
  value hypothesis metrics forward.
- Preserve the champion relationship. The champion is the cheapest expansion
  motion you have.
- Document the expansion path: the next metric to improve, and the next buyer
  to sell to.

**In-channel (partners / VARs)**

- The partner executes delivery and implementation where they own it.
- The partner's margin is confirmed and fulfillment starts on schedule.
- Include the partner in the handoff so the account relationship survives the
  transition from sales to delivery.

**Resources sellers need**

- A transition checklist and a kickoff template.
- The customer-success transition checklist and onboarding kit.

**Artifacts this stage produces**

- A signed deal plus a handoff document: what was promised, the success
  metrics, the stakeholders, and the next expansion step.
- A kickoff agenda template and an EBR (executive business review) template.

**Exit criteria (hard gate)**

- [ ] All MEDDPICC letters are filled and evidenced.
- [ ] All sales artifacts are uploaded to the CRM (Salesforce).
- [ ] A customer kickoff call is scheduled; deployment objectives are
      documented.
- [ ] The account team has met internally to ensure a smooth handoff; the PO /
      signed agreement is attached.
- [ ] The win is announced on the close path; customer success now owns the
      technical relationship.

## Templates: resources sellers use

A resource is reusable reference. These four ship with the flow.

### Territory plan template

```
Territory plan: <region / segment>          as-of: <date>
Named ICP: <one line>
Quota this period: <$>     Pipeline needed: <$ at 3-4x quota>
Top plays this quarter:
  1. <play>
  2. <play>
  3. <play>
Top 5 accounts:
  1. <account> | <pain> | <champion/EB> | <value $> | <next step>
  2. <account> | ...
  3. <account> | ...
  4. <account> | ...
  5. <account> | ...
```

The pipeline number is not a guess. To hold $100k of quota you need roughly
$300k-$400k of qualified pipeline, because pipeline coverage of 3-4x is the
healthy band for mid-market (getSpike SaaS benchmarks, 2026). Fill the top 5
rows before you add row 6.

### PG 5x5 grid

Five accounts by five columns, refreshed every week. It keeps the territory
plan honest by forcing a next step on the five deals that matter most.

```
| Account | Pain | Champion / EB | Value $ | Next step (date + owner) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acct 1 | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| Acct 2 | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| Acct 3 | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| Acct 4 | ... | ... | ... | ... |
| Acct 5 | ... | ... | ... | ... |
```

If a row has no next step, the deal is stalled, and the grid makes it visible
instead of letting it hide in the CRM.

### First-meeting deck skeleton

A 30-minute first meeting, owned by the buyer talking.

```
0-3    Why we are here + your goal for the call
3-10   Your current state (buyer talks, you ask)
10-20  The problem and its cost, quantified
20-28  What would change + a first value hypothesis
28-30  Next step + who else needs to be involved
```

No product tour in a first meeting. The deck exists to move the buyer to a
discovery conversation with the right people in the room.

### Value hypothesis template (the artifact)

This is an artifact: a deliverable produced for a specific deal, updated at
each stage, and the internal contract the deal is measured against.

```
Value Hypothesis
Account:              <name>
Buyer / role:         <name, title>
Pain (their words):   <quote>
Cost of pain:         <$ per period, the buyer's number>   as-of: <date>
Desired outcome:      <metric + target + timeline>
Decision criteria:    <criteria they named>
Decision process:     <steps + people>
Paper process:        <procurement / legal / security>
Economic buyer:       <name, title, confirmed access: yes/no>
Champion:             <name, title>
Competition:          <status quo + alternatives>
Deal value:           <$ ACV>                              as-of: <date>
Next step + owner + date: <...>
```

The difference from a resource: the value hypothesis is written for one deal,
in the buyer's numbers, with an as-of date. A blank template is a resource.
A filled one is an artifact, and it is what gates the stage.

## A worked deal, end to end

Acme, a mid-market marketing ops team, $50k ACV. Buy-side buyer is the VP of
Operations.

**Stage 1 - Qualification.** Pain: revenue reporting is wrong and the team
rebuilds it manually. Economic buyer: VP Ops. Budget: a line exists. Status
quo: spreadsheets and a consultant. Qualifies.

**Stage 2 - Discovery.** The VP estimates the manual rebuild costs about two
hours a day. Two hours x 250 working days x $60/hour burden is about $30k a
year of wasted time. That is the buyer's number, on their base. Decision
criteria: accuracy, time to report, and cost. Champion emerging: the RevOps
lead, who owns the rebuild.

**Stage 3 - Scoping.** Decision process: RevOps lead evaluates, VP Ops
approves, Finance signs. Paper process: a security review that takes three
weeks, discovered now rather than at proposal. Value sized at $30k/year of
recovered time plus the accuracy fix.

**Stage 4 - Evaluating.** Champion: the RevOps lead. Evaluation: a pilot on
one report, measured against the decision criteria. Competition: the
incumbent consultant. Your differentiator: the pilot beats the consultant's
timeline.

**Stage 5 - Proposal and Negotiation.** Proposal maps to the three criteria.
List is $50k. Procurement asks for 20% off. You hold price and add a $3k
services credit instead, which costs less margin than the 20% discount.
Economic buyer agrees. Security review completes on its named timeline.

**Stage 6 - Closed-Won.** Signed at $50k, PO issued, handoff written with the
$30k/year and accuracy metrics. Expansion path: the next report the team
wants fixed.

**The arithmetic you should trust.** At $50k ACV (2026 base) and a 21% close
rate, one win needs about five qualified opportunities. Five qualified opps
times a 21% close rate is just over one win. The ~21% Opp to Closed-Won
figure is Ebsta/Pavilion (2021-2024). SalesMotion (2026) puts qualified win
rates near 29%, which still means three to four qualified opps per win. Hold
four qualified opps and expect to lose one. That is a named base, not a
guess. Quota is a pipeline problem before it is a closing problem.

## Failure modes and how to fix them

- **Advancing on a miss.** Skipping an exit gate feels like speed and costs
  the next stage. A deal that reaches proposal without a named economic buyer
  gets discounted by procurement. Fix: the gate is a hard stop.
- **No economic buyer until negotiation.** If you do not know who says yes,
  procurement will negotiate against a ghost. Fix: name the EB in
  qualification and re-confirm before proposal.
- **No champion, or a champion found too late.** A deal without an internal
  seller dies in evaluation. Fix: identify the champion by discovery and
  develop them through evaluation with ammunition they can use.
- **Paper process discovered at proposal.** A security review found at
  signature adds weeks you cannot afford. Fix: start procurement in scoping.
- **Value never quantified.** A deal sold on features is a deal negotiated on
  price. Fix: write the value hypothesis with the buyer's numbers by
  discovery, as-of dated.
- **Partner margin conflict surfaced too late.** A VAR that discovers its
  margin is thin at signature withdraws or undercuts. Fix: deal registration
  and aligned margins in qualification and scoping, not at the close.

## How the flow differs by segment and maturity

- **By deal size.** Under $20k ACV, stages 3 and 4 compress into a shorter
  evaluation, and the paper process is light. At $250k and up, expect a
  heavier paper process, more stakeholders, and champions at multiple levels.
- **By segment.** SMB is often product-led and self-serve, with a short
  assisted motion. Mid-market runs the classic six stages. Enterprise adds
  committee selling, where the champion must win multiple evaluators.
- **By maturity.** A new motion with few wins should qualify hard and chase
  only winnable deals, because every lost deal is also a lost learning cycle.
  A mature motion with a track record can afford pipeline breadth.
- **Channel-led deals.** When a partner owns the relationship, stages compress
  for you, but you still verify the MEDDPICC letters yourself. Never trust
  the partner's read on economic buyer or champion. Confirm it on the call.

## Agentic layer

An agent can run the two most mechanical parts of this flow: writing the
value hypothesis from a discovery transcript, and checking the exit gate
against the notes. Both keep a deal from advancing on a miss.

**Prompt: write a value hypothesis from a discovery call.**

```
You are a sales process operator. Given a discovery call transcript and any
deal notes, produce a Value Hypothesis in this exact structure:
- Pain (buyer's words, quoted)
- Cost of pain (the buyer's number, with the math they stated, as-of dated)
- Desired outcome (metric + target + timeline)
- Decision criteria (as named)
- Decision process (steps + people)
- Paper process (procurement / legal / security)
- Economic buyer (name, title, confirmed access: yes/no)
- Champion (name, title)
- Competition (status quo + alternatives)
- Deal value ($ ACV)
For each MEDDPICC letter, state CONFIRMED, UNCONFIRMED, or MISSING, and the
one next action to move it to CONFIRMED. Quote only what is in the
transcript. Anything not stated is MISSING. Do not invent numbers.
```

**SOP: run the stage gate.**

```
For the current stage, load the exit criteria. Read the deal notes and value
hypothesis. For each criterion, mark MET or NOT MET with a one-line evidence
reference. If any criterion is NOT MET, do not advance the deal: emit a
blocked notice listing the missing evidence and the owner + date to close it.
If all criteria are MET, advance the stage and emit the updated value
hypothesis for the next stage. Never advance a deal on a miss.
```

This is the same discipline as the rest of the wiki: the flow gates the deal,
the agent enforces the gate, and a deal only moves when the evidence is on
record. A stalled deal becomes visible in the notes, not a surprise at
proposal.
