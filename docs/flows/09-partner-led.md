# Partner-Led Flow - the Partner Pipeline

[Partner / Channel](../channels/partner) states the channel argument, the four partner types, and the comp economics. This page states the run: the ordered steps from a decision gate to a scaled partner channel, with one artifact that feeds the next.

Two rules hold the flow together:

- **Activation, not signings, is the score.** A signed partner who never launches contributes nothing. Average affiliate programs sit near 10 percent activation, and programs with strong onboarding reach 50 percent or higher [128][96].
- **Pay for closed revenue, not for the introduction.** A partner who sends leads that never close burns your pipeline hygiene. Tie comp to closed-won deals [95].

## Pipeline at a glance

The partner flow is six steps run in order. Each step emits one artifact that feeds the next.

| # | Step | Artifact you must have before advancing |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | Decision gate | A product past PMF, a partner type chosen, a shared ICP confirmed |
| 1 | Qualify partners | A 4C scorecard with customer base, credibility, capability, commitment |
| 2 | Comp model | A written rate card with an attribution rule agreed before the first deal |
| 3 | Onboard | A 30/60/90 plan with one exit condition per phase |
| 4 | First registered deal | A partner-sourced opportunity logged with source and partner ID |
| 5 | Attribution loop | A monthly read of activation, sourced pipeline, and cost per lead |
| 6 | Scale or retire | A written decision per partner type, read against the cost math |

## Step 0 - Decision gate

Run this flow when three conditions hold at once.

**The product has distribution already.** A partner amplifies a market that exists. A pre-PMF product has no proof a partner can sell, so run the direct motion first [220].

**A partner type is chosen.** Integration, referral, reseller, or channel. Each type carries a different comp model and a different definition of activation. Mixing them produces the category error the channel page warns about.

**The ICPs overlap.** The partner's customer base must sit on your ICP. No shared customer, no deal at the end of the partnership.

## Step 1 - Qualify the first partners

Recruitment decides whether a partner enters at all. A weak qualification here makes onboarding a fix that arrives too late [96].

Every target clears the 4C gate:

1. **Customer base.** The partner's customers overlap your ICP.
2. **Credibility.** The partner's brand carries trust with the buyers you want.
3. **Capability.** The partner can sell, implement, or deliver the product.
4. **Commitment.** A named owner, a joint plan, and a revenue target.

Log each target in a row with company, partner type, ICP-overlap score (1 to 5), named contact, the joint motion you will build, and the comp model on offer. No joint motion, no outreach.

## Step 2 - Design the comp model

Comp decides how much attention a partner gives your product. The channel page carries the rate table. This step writes the version you will use.

| Partner type | What it pays | Typical rate (as of 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Referral / affiliate | First-year value share | About 20 percent of first-year value [204] |
| Revenue share on recurring | Monthly recurring net revenue | About 20 percent of MRR, paid up to three years |
| Reseller margin | Discount off list price | 20 to 30 percent [152] |
| Marketplace / distributor | Cut of each transaction | 15 to 25 percent |

Three rules make the model work:

- **One source of truth on attribution.** Only 42 percent of companies use multi-touch attribution. The rest guess, and guessing breaks trust [95].
- **Tie comp to partner tier.** Co-sell partners who close earn more than partners who only refer.
- **Do not discount for a partner who brings no deals.** A price cut with no registered deal is a margin cut you made alone.

## Step 3 - Onboard on the 30/60/90

Onboarding is the bounded first 90 days after signing. It ends when the partner can sell solo, not when the calendar runs out [96].

| Phase | Days | Goal | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alignment and access | 1 to 30 | Partner states your value in their own words | Partner records your positioning in their own words |
| First proof | 31 to 60 | Demo, first co-sell, first deal registration | Partner logs a qualified opportunity |
| Solo motion | 61 to 90 | Partner sells solo | Partner runs the full motion without you on the call |

Training completion and portal logins are not activation. The milestone is an outcome: a recorded positioning, a demo delivered solo, a qualified opportunity logged [128].

## Step 4 - Register and source the first deal

Deal registration is the instrument that makes the channel measurable. A partner-sourced deal that never enters the system is a number you cannot read.

Write the qualified-partner-meeting standard before the first deal. If you cannot name who counts, "partner-sourced meetings" means nothing.

Require four fields on every registered deal: partner ID, the partner type, the deal stage, and the attribution rule applied. No source field, no scale.

## Step 5 - Run the attribution loop

Read the funnel each month, in this order: activation, sourced pipeline, cost per lead.

| Stage | Healthy range | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Partner activation rate | Above 50 percent of signed partners launch [128] | Whether onboarding worked |
| Partner-sourced pipeline | 10 to 20 percent of new pipeline | Whether the channel matters [220] |
| Referral conversion lift | About 30 percent higher than other channels | Whether referral leads pay [55] |
| Affiliate MRR contribution | 10 to 20 percent of MRR | Whether the economics hold [49] |
| Cost per acquired lead | 30 to 40 percent lower than paid ads | The core economic argument [134] |

Activation is the gate. Optimizing pipeline share while partners have not launched measures noise.

## Step 6 - Scale or retire

Scale a partner type when activation holds above 50 percent and sourced pipeline reaches 10 percent of new pipeline. Retire a partner type when signed partners stay below 30 percent activation after two onboarding cycles, because that pattern signals a recruitment or onboarding-design problem rather than a market problem [96].

## Worked example

Take a B2B SaaS product at $2,400 per seat per year, priced as of 2026, with a 30 percent reseller margin off list price [152].

A referral partner sends five closed deals in a quarter. Each deal is $24,000 in first-year value, and the comp rate is 20 percent of first-year value, so the partner earns $24,000 per quarter [204].

Paid search on the same product costs about $802 per customer, and partnership-sourced leads cost 30 to 40 percent less, so the partner channel sits near $500 per lead [134].

Referred customers convert about 30 percent better and carry about 16 percent higher lifetime value, which adds roughly $3,840 per referred customer on a $24,000 first-year deal [61][82].

The comp bill is $24,000 per quarter against $120,000 in first-year value. The channel costs 20 cents per dollar of sourced revenue, and the referral lift carries forward into the renewal base.

## How the motion differs by stage

| Stage | One partner type to start | Comp model | Human owner | Success gate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seed / founder-led | One referral partner (a customer fan) | Non-monetary plus a small referral cut | Founder tracks the relationship | First closed deal traces to the partner |
| Early team (Series A to B) | One integration partner plus one referral | Revenue share on closed deals | One partner manager, half time | Activation above 50 percent [128] |
| Mid-market (Series C+) | Integration, referral, reseller lane | Tiered comp by partner type | Dedicated partner manager | Sourced pipeline reaches 10 percent [220] |
| Enterprise | Full portfolio: integration, referral, reseller, channel | Multi-tier comp with MDF | Partner ops plus dedicated reps | 35 percent partner-influenced pipeline [95] |

A seed founder who signs five partners spreads attention across five relationships with no proven product to offer. A nine-month sales cycle reads sourced pipeline instead of a referral coefficient, because the loop mechanism does not fit an enterprise motion [61].

## Failure modes

- **Signings as the score.** A logo feels like progress, so the team reports partner count instead of sourced pipeline.
- **The integration nobody asked for.** The integration becomes the goal, and the shared customer never arrives.
- **No attribution rule.** The team guesses who gets credit, and the partner relationship dies on the second dispute.
- **A discount for nothing.** The team cuts price for a partner who brings no registered deal.
- **One generic onboarding.** A referral partner and a reseller get the same track, and both stall in phase one.
- **Scaling before activation.** The team opens the program before the first partners launch, and the broken onboarding runs at full size.
- **The services partner who owns the customer.** The comp sits on the services fee instead of the recurring product, so the incentive points away from your revenue.

## Agentic layer

An agent watches the partner funnel and drafts the sourcing list. A human approves every outreach and every attribution decision.

```
Monthly, for every signed partner:
  1. Pull activation, sourced pipeline, and cost per lead per partner
     type and per partner.
  2. Flag the partners below the 30 percent activation line and the
     ones with no registered deal in two cycles.
  3. Draft the reactivation or retirement note, with the evidence
     attached.
  4. Check every registered deal against the agreed attribution rule
     before any commission is calculated.
  5. If no partner broke its range, say so and stop.
Never auto-pay a commission without a closed-won deal.
```

An agent that auto-pays burns margin. An agent that auto-blocks chases off real partners. Keep the payout decision with a person, and read [Agent vs Human Workflows](../agentic/agent-vs-human-workflows) for the autonomy placement.
