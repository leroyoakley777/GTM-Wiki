# Inspection and Deal Review

A revenue team repeats what it inspects. Inspection is the weekly habit of reading real work, the deals, the calls, and the notes, against one written standard. Deal review is the part of that habit that touches live opportunities.

Most teams inspect the dashboard and call it management. A dashboard shows what a rep typed. Inspection shows what a buyer said. When the two disagree, the typed number wins the meeting and loses the quarter.

This page is the operator view. It pairs with [Culture](/docs/culture) for the habits behind it and [Heads of GTM](/docs/roles/heads-of-gtm) for the seat that runs the review.

## What inspection and deal review govern

Inspection decides four things a CRM field cannot:

- **Which deals get attention.** A rep marks a deal "commit" and the number moves. Inspection asks what the buyer did this week, not what the rep expects next week.
- **Whether the stage is real.** A deal at "proposal" with no named economic buyer is a label, not a stage. Teams that run a defined forecasting analysis hit quota 28% more often than teams that forecast on judgment alone <sup><a href="#sources">[160]</a></sup>.
- **Where the skill gap sits.** One deal shows a missed question. Ten deals show a missing discovery habit, and the fix is coaching, not a new tool <sup><a href="#sources">[262]</a></sup>.
- **What the number is worth.** A forecast built on typed stages drifts. Only 20% of sales organizations forecast within 5% of plan, and 43% miss goal by 10% or more <sup><a href="#sources">[358]</a></sup>.

Forecast accuracy bands tell the same story from the other side. Average teams land between 50% and 70%, and top-decile teams land between 80% and 95% <sup><a href="#sources">[137]</a></sup>. Fewer than 20% of sales leaders rate their own forecast predictable <sup><a href="#sources">[157]</a></sup>. Those numbers describe the output. Inspection is the input.

## Weekly inspection cadence

Inspection is a calendar item, not a mood. Run it the same week, every week.

| Cadence | What to inspect | Evidence to demand | Owner |
|---------|-----------------|--------------------|-------|
| Daily | New replies and booked meetings | The reply text, not the count | SDR lead |
| Weekly | Ten pieces of real work: emails, calls, demos | The artifact, plus the outcome it produced | Frontline manager |
| Weekly | Every deal in commit | The buyer action from this week | AE and manager |
| Monthly | Loss codes on closed deals | The coded reason, not rep memory | RevOps |
| Quarterly | ICP and disqualifiers | Win rates by segment | GTM leadership |

The weekly deal review carries one rule: no deal advances a stage without a buyer action that justifies the move. A "verbal yes" is not a buyer action. A signed order form, a scheduled security review, or a named budget line is.

## How the cadence changes by stage

- **Seed.** The founder reviews every open deal. The base is small, so each conversation teaches the ICP. A pipeline review is a founder reading notes aloud.
- **First sales team.** One manager reviews the full pipeline weekly and reads ten work samples. The standard lives in a written checklist, not in one person's memory.
- **Growth.** Split the review. The manager owns deals, enablement owns skill, and RevOps owns the definition of a qualified stage <sup><a href="#sources">[299]</a></sup>.
- **Scale.** Review by segment and sample work instead of reading every deal. Automate the data pull so the meeting spends its hour on decisions, not on assembling a report.

## Failure modes and fixes

- **Dashboard theater.** The meeting reads a screen. Fix: open the call recording and the email thread for the same deal <sup><a href="#sources">[336]</a></sup>.
- **Status-report review.** Reps recite a summary. Fix: ask one question, "what did the buyer do since we last spoke," and wait for a specific act.
- **Stage inflation.** A deal moves to "negotiation" with no legal or procurement contact. Fix: tie every stage to a buyer action and an exit criterion.
- **Forecast as a feeling.** The commit number comes from optimism. Fix: review the evidence behind each commit deal, then roll the number up from those deals <sup><a href="#sources">[160]</a></sup>.
- **Coaching every skill at once.** The manager corrects five things and the rep remembers none. Fix: pick one skill for the week <sup><a href="#sources">[262]</a></sup>.
- **Inspection without a standard.** The manager judges from taste. Fix: write the checklist first, then inspect against it.
- **Punishing the bad news.** A rep hides a slipped deal to avoid the conversation. Fix: reward the early flag and diagnose the slip in the open <sup><a href="#sources">[279]</a></sup>.

## Worked example: a pipeline review that changes a number

A 12-rep team carries $4.2M in commit for a $1.0M quarter. Coverage looks healthy on the slide. Win rate runs 21% <sup><a href="#sources">[144]</a></sup>, so the team needs roughly 4.8x coverage on qualified pipeline <sup><a href="#sources">[112]</a></sup>. The review reads ten commit deals against the stage rules.

The review finds five deals that fail the rule: three sit at "proposal" with no economic buyer engaged, and two show a last buyer action a month old. It recodes those five as at risk. Commit drops to $2.9M, and coverage falls under the 4.8x the 21% win rate demands <sup><a href="#sources">[112]</a></sup>. The manager adds the five deals to next week's working list, assigns one discovery question to each, and sets a buyer-action gate for every stage. The number changed because the evidence changed, not because the rep worked harder.

## Inspection checklist

```text
WEEKLY INSPECTION CHECKLIST
[ ] Ten work samples read against the current standard
[ ] Every commit deal carries one buyer action from this week
[ ] Every stage has an exit criterion a buyer can satisfy
[ ] No deal advanced on a "verbal yes"
[ ] One skill picked for coaching, not five
[ ] Slipped deals surfaced early and diagnosed in the open
[ ] Loss codes reviewed against the taxonomy, not rep memory
[ ] Forecast rolled up from evidence, not from optimism
```

## Run it this week

One. Pick the ten largest commit deals. For each, write the last buyer action and its date. A deal with no action in two weeks moves to the watch list.

Two. Take the deal with the weakest evidence and run a short review in front of the team. The questions: what did the buyer do, who else weighs in, what almost stopped it, what is the next buyer action, and what would make us walk away.

Three. Write the exit criterion for the stage where deals stall. Publish it. Review against it next week.

## Agentic layer

An agent can pull the deal fields, diff last week against this week, and flag any deal that advanced a stage with no new buyer activity. It can summarize call recordings into a one-line buyer action per deal. A human still runs the review and owns the coaching. Set the guardrail first: the agent reads and flags, and never changes a stage or a forecast number. See [Agentic outbound](/docs/agentic/agentic-outbound) for the desk-level SOP.
