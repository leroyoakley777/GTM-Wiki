# AEs

The account executive owns the close. An AE takes a qualified opportunity and
turns it into revenue. The job runs from the first discovery call to the signed
contract, and it includes the forecast the whole company bets on.

This page is the operator's guide to the role. It covers what the job is, the
numbers that gate it, how to run the process, and the mistakes that kill deals
in the last quarter.

---

## Definition

An account executive (AE) closes deals. The AE owns the opportunity once an SDR
or marketing has qualified it. The AE runs discovery, builds the business case,
coordinates the buying committee, works through procurement, and gets the signature
(see [SDRs](../sdrs/index.md) for the handoff that feeds this role).

The role splits into two common shapes:

- **New business AE.** Closes net-new logos. Owns the full sales cycle from a
  qualified lead to a signed customer. Usually measured on new ARR.
- **Expansion AE.** Closes growth inside existing accounts. Sells upgrades,
  additional seats, and new modules. Measured on expansion revenue.

Some teams run a **full-cycle** model where one AE owns acquisition and
expansion in a territory. Others split new business and growth into separate
desks. The split depends on deal size and account complexity. Enterprise teams
usually specialize. SMB teams usually run full cycle.

The AE is distinct from the SDR. An SDR finds and qualifies. An AE closes and
forecasts. When the two blur, prospecting time collapses and the close gets
starved. See [SDRs](../sdrs/index.md) and [founders](../founders/index.md) for
when to build each desk.

## Why it matters

The AE is the revenue point of conversion. Everything upstream feeds the close,
and the close is where pipeline becomes cash. Without a strong AE desk, an
excellent pipeline machine converts nothing.

The numbers are honest about the difficulty. B2B win rates run about 20 to 21
percent on average (Ebsta via Pavilion, 2024). Qualified opportunities close at
a higher rate, around 29 percent in one 2026 benchmark (SalesMotion, 2026). The
gap between those two numbers is the qualification quality upstream and the AE's
execution downstream.

Win rates vary by segment. SMB deals win at roughly 31 percent, while enterprise
deals above a high ACV win at about 15 percent (SalesMotion, 2026). Enterprise
deals are harder, longer, and involve more people. The AE who closes them earns
the margin, but the odds are worse.

Quota attainment is low across the industry. Roughly 43 to 47 percent of reps
hit quota (RepVue, Q4 2024). SalesFit puts the industry average near 47 percent
(SalesFit, 2025). That means the majority of AEs miss quota in most quarters.
This is a structural number, not a talent judgment. The teams that beat it do
so with a disciplined process, not heroics.

The buying environment makes the job harder, not easier. Buyers do most of the
journey before they contact a seller. Roughly two-thirds of the buying journey
happens before the first vendor engagement, and about 80 percent of deals go to
the vendor on the day-one shortlist (6sense, 2025). The AE does not win most
deals. The AE keeps a deal from being lost by a stronger pre-existing favorite.

## Metrics that gate the role

Measure the AE on outcomes, not activity. The AE's activity (calls, emails,
demos) matters less than what converts. Use these numbers to run the desk.

| Metric | What it tells you | Benchmark |
|--------|-------------------|-----------|
| Win rate | Deals closed against opportunities worked | ~20-21% all, ~29% qualified |
| Quota attainment | Reps hitting their number | ~43-47% industry |
| Sales cycle length | Time from open to close | Varies by segment |
| Pipeline coverage | Open pipeline against quota | 3-4x mid, 4-5x enterprise |
| Average deal size | Revenue per closed deal | Varies by ACV |
| Forecast accuracy | Reliability of the number given | 50-70% average, 80-95% top |
| Disqualification rate | Speed of killing bad deals | Track, no fixed floor |

The most important number is win rate on qualified opportunities. This is the
AE's conversion efficiency. It tells you whether the rep is executing discovery,
building a business case, and navigating the committee, or just presenting.

Average deal size tells you the type of motion you run. A $10K ACV desk is a
volume game. A $500K ACV desk is a relationship and process game. The skills
overlap, but the cadence and the committee management differ sharply.

Pipeline coverage tells you whether the quarter is winnable. Mid-market teams
need 3 to 4 times open pipeline against quota. Enterprise teams need 4 to 5
times (getSpike, 2026). Below that, the win rate has no room to absorb loss.
At a 21 percent win rate you need roughly 4.8 times coverage to be safe
(Lative, 2025).

Forecast accuracy is the number leadership actually feels. Average teams land
within 50 to 70 percent of forecast. Top-decile teams land within 80 to 95
percent (Forecastio, 2025). The gap is the difference between a credible number
and a hope. See [RevOps](../revops/index.md) for the forecasting system behind
it.

## Framework: the close as a system

The AE does not wing the close. The close runs on a framework that maps the
buying committee and the decision. The standard is MEDDPICC.

Qualification frameworks split into two jobs. Some are fast filters for early
conversations. Others are deal-management systems for closers. The AE needs the
second kind (SalesHive, 2026). MEDDPICC is the most thorough of the
deal-management frameworks and the most demanding. Use it.

MEDDPICC stands for:

- **Metrics.** The quantified business impact the buyer cares about. Without a
  number, there is no case.
- **Economic Buyer.** The person who controls the money. Find them early.
- **Decision Criteria.** How the buyer will evaluate options. Write down their
  exact criteria and beat each one.
- **Decision Process.** The steps and approvals required. Map the path from here
  to signature.
- **Identify Pain.** The compelling reason to change. Pain must be real, felt,
  and quantified.
- **Champion.** An internal advocate who sells for you. One champion is not
  enough in enterprise; you need a coalition.
- **Paper Process.** Procurement, legal, and security review steps. Enterprise
  deals die here more than anywhere else.
- **Competition.** Who else the buyer considers, including the status quo. You
  cannot beat what you refuse to name.

The two extra letters, Paper Process and Competition, exist because enterprise
deals die in procurement and against incumbents (SalesHive, 2026). The deal does
not end at the economic buyer's yes. It ends when legal signs.

The runnable version of this lives in the
[sales process flow](../../flows/sales-process-meddpicc): six stage-gated stages
from qualification to closed-won, with exit criteria and the value hypothesis
artifact. Run that flow. Do not reinvent the stages per deal.

The framework's job is forecasting. MEDDIC was built at PTC in the 1990s for
complex enterprise software. It lets an AE look at a six-figure deal and say
precisely what is known, what is unknown, and what the path to signature is
(SalesHive, 2026). That clarity is what makes the forecast credible.

## How to execute

### 1. Qualify hard on entry

The close starts with qualification, not with the demo. Before you run any
discovery, decide whether the deal deserves your time. Apply the ICP filters
first. A deal with no economic buyer, no pain, and no budget is not a deal, it
is a meeting. See [ICP definition](../../foundations/02-icp-definition.md).

Disqualify fast. The cost of a bad deal is not just the lost hours. It is the
opportunity cost of the good deals you did not work. The best AEs are brutal
about killing deals early. See the [sales process flow](../../flows/sales-process-meddpicc)
for the disqualification gates.

### 2. Run discovery to build the case

Discovery is where the deal is won or lost. The AE's job is to map the metrics,
the pain, the decision criteria, and the process, in that order. Ask questions.
Quantify everything. A pain that is not a number is not a case.

Buyers are more likely to buy when their goals are understood. About 86 percent
of business buyers say they are more likely to purchase when the seller
understands their goals (Salesforce, 2024). Discovery is that understanding
turned into a written business case.

### 3. Build the value hypothesis

Turn the discovery into a value hypothesis: the quantified outcome the buyer
will get, the cost of inaction, and the proof. Write it down. Show it to the
champion. Get them to correct it. The value hypothesis is the backbone of every
later conversation. The flow's artifact is the template for it. See the
[sales process flow](../../flows/sales-process-meddpicc).

### 4. Map and work the committee

Enterprise decisions involve many people. Buying decisions span multiple
departments and roughly a dozen or more participants in many cases (Forrester,
2024). Sellers do not talk to all of them, and that is the point of the
champion. Map the committee, find the economic buyer, and arm your champion to
sell for you.

Understand the buyer roles. In any buying group there are mobilizers who push
the change, talkers who discuss it, and blockers who resist it. Sellers who
target the mobilizers are far more likely to be high performers (CEB
Challenger, 2015). Find the person with a reason to change and sell to them.

### 5. Compete on the process, not the pitch

Name the competition. Too many AEs refuse to ask who else is in the deal and
lose to the status quo by default. Ask early who the buyer is considering,
including doing nothing. If the answer is the status quo, your case must beat
inertia, not a rival.

ABM data shows why this matters. Teams running account-based motion against the
buying group see higher win rates than those selling to single contacts
(SalesMotion, 2026). Engage the committee, not the champion alone.

### 6. Work through procurement and close

The final stretch is procurement, legal, and security. This is where enterprise
deals die. Start the paper process early, not after the economic yes. Send the
security questionnaire before the business case is final. Line up the champion
to push the approval through. A deal that clears business but dies in legal is
a process failure, not a loss.

### 7. Forecast like the company depends on it

The AE's forecast is a commitment, not a wish. Forecast only what you can prove
with the MEDDPICC data. If you have no paper process started and no economic
buyer confirmed, the deal does not belong in the committed bucket. See
[RevOps](../revops/index.md) for the discipline behind it.

## Weekly cadence

A new-business AE runs a fixed rhythm. Most of the week goes to the active deal
queue: discovery, demos, follow-ups, and committee calls. A protected block goes
to building the next quarter, usually a few hours a day of prospecting and
account research. The AE who lives only in the current pipeline has a cold next
quarter.

Pipeline building is not the SDR's job alone. The AE owns the relationships and
the territory knowledge. A strong AE spends a share of the week opening new
conversations inside target accounts. This is not the SDR's volume motion, it
is a targeted executive-to-executive motion.

The rest of the week is forecast maintenance and internal alignment: keeping
the CRM current, updating the deal stages, and aligning with RevOps and
leadership on the number. This is the unglamorous 30 percent that makes the
forecast credible.

## Agentic layer

The AE role is changing at the edges, not the core. AI handles the research and
the busywork. The judgment stays with the human.

The buying committee research that used to take hours now takes minutes. AI
assembles account briefs, surfaces triggers, and drafts follow-ups. See
[agentic outbound](../../agentic/02-agentic-outbound.md) and the
[agentic stack](../../agentic/01-agentic-stack.md) for the tools behind this.

The close itself stays human. Buying is a relationship and a committee process.
The mobilizer, the economic buyer, and the champion need a person they trust.
AI drafts the email, but the relationship runs through the AE.

The discipline is to use AI for throughput, not for the decision. Let the tools
build the brief. Keep the strategy, the discovery, and the relationship on the
human. See [agent vs human workflows](../../agentic/06-agent-vs-human-workflows.md)
for where the line sits.

## Career path

The AE desk sits between the SDR motion and leadership.

- **From SDR.** The SDR who can qualify, research, and communicate becomes the
  AE. The pipeline skills are the same skills, one stage later.
- **To leadership.** Strong AEs move to team lead, VP of sales, or head of GTM.
  The reps who forecast cleanly and coach others are the candidates. See
  [vps-sales](../vps-sales/index.md) and [heads-of-gtm](../heads-of-gtm/index.md).
- **To specialization.** Some AEs move into enterprise accounts, partner sales,
  or platform ecosystems. Deal size and complexity scale up with the segment.

The role compounds. An AE who learns to build business cases, work committees,
and forecast with discipline becomes more valuable in every segment.
The skills transfer across industries and products. That is why strong AEs are
always in demand.

## Common mistakes

### 1. Presenting instead of discovering

The biggest AE mistake is treating the demo as the pitch. The deal is won in
discovery, not the demo. If you are presenting features before you have mapped
the metrics and the process, you are losing. Run discovery first.

### 2. Chasing the champion only

One champion is not a deal. Enterprise decisions span departments and dozens of
participants (Forrester, 2024). If the champion is the only person you know, the
economic buyer is a surprise waiting to kill the deal. Map the committee.

### 3. Refusing to name the competition

Losing to the status quo by default is the most common silent loss. Ask who
else is in the deal, including doing nothing. You cannot beat what you refuse to
name.

### 4. Ignoring paper process until the end

The economic buyer says yes and the deal dies in legal. Enterprise deals die in
procurement. Start the paper process early, not after the business case.

### 5. Forecasting hope instead of data

A forecast with no paper process and no confirmed economic buyer is a wish.
Leadership relies on your number. If you pad it, the company spends against
revenue that does not exist. See [RevOps](../revops/index.md).

### 6. Treating every deal the same

A $10K deal and a $500K deal run on different machinery. Volume motion for SMB,
process and committee motion for enterprise. Applying the wrong cadence to the
wrong deal size wastes both.

### 7. No pipeline for next quarter

Living only in the current pipeline starves the next quarter. Protect time for
account building every week. The AE who opens zero new conversations has a cold
future.
