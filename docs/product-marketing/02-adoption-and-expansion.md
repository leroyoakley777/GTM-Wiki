# Adoption and expansion

A launch puts a capability in front of the market. Adoption turns that capability into a habit, and expansion turns the habit into more revenue from the same account. Product marketing owns both halves, because the same claim that earns the first use has to earn the second and the tenth.

The economics set the stakes. Median net revenue retention for SaaS sits near 102 percent, and top performers draw more than 60 percent of new MRR from expansion <sup><a href="#sources">[156]</a></sup>. A public SaaS company that holds net dollar retention above 110 percent carries a higher valuation than one that does not <sup><a href="#sources">[315]</a></sup>. Growth from the installed base costs less than growth from new logos, and it starts with adoption.

This page covers how to name and measure adoption, how to drive it after the launch, and how to run the expansion motion. Pair it with [Product marketing](/docs/product-marketing) for the function, [Launch and message testing](./launch-and-messaging) for the launch that precedes it, [Customer onboarding](/docs/playbooks/customer-onboarding) for the activation mechanics, and [GTM metrics](/docs/foundations/gtm-metrics) for the retention numbers.

## What adoption and expansion govern

Adoption and expansion are two stages of the same job. Adoption asks whether the customer reached the value the product promises. Expansion asks whether that value grew into more seats, more usage, or a higher tier.

| Stage | The question | Product marketing owns | Signal |
|-------|--------------|------------------------|--------|
| Activation | Did the customer reach first value? | The activation event and the in-product path to it | Day-7 return, time to value |
| Adoption | Do they use it the way the claim promised? | Release notes, in-product messaging, adoption campaigns | Feature usage by cohort |
| Expansion | Did the value grow into more revenue? | The expansion play, the packaging, the proof | Net revenue retention, expansion MRR |

Brand still carries the demand side. Only about 5 percent of B2B buyers are in-market at a given time, so the memory a launch builds is what a future expansion decision draws on <sup><a href="#sources">[312]</a></sup>. Adoption and expansion are where that memory meets the product.

## Measure adoption before you scale it

Adoption work without a defined activation event is guesswork. Pick the one action that predicts a customer who stays, then measure against it.

Test the activation event before you trust it. A named event earns its place when retention diverges for the cohort that reaches it, when the pattern holds across segments, and when moving the number moves revenue <sup><a href="#sources">[237]</a></sup>. Completion rate is a compliance metric, not an activation metric <sup><a href="#sources">[237]</a></sup>.

The day-7 return is the earliest honest signal. Top-quartile products bring about 7 percent of a signup cohort back on day seven, and more than 98 percent of users who never reach value churn within two weeks <sup><a href="#sources">[235]</a></sup>. Median B2B SaaS activation sits near 37 percent, so most of a signup base never reaches first value <sup><a href="#sources">[236]</a></sup>. Those two numbers set the adoption job: widen the path to the activation event and shorten the time it takes.

## Drive adoption after the launch

Adoption marketing starts the day the launch narrative ships. The work is to put the claim in front of the people who already hold the product, at the moment it becomes true for them.

- **Name the behavior, not the feature.** A release note that describes the capability in the buyer's language earns the click; a changelog of internals does not <sup><a href="#sources">[224]</a></sup>.
- **Put the claim where the work happens.** In-product tours, empty states, and lifecycle email carry the message to the user who is already inside the product.
- **Show one idea per asset.** A launch, a release note, and an in-product tip each carry one idea, because a message that asks the reader to hold several at once loses all of them <sup><a href="#sources">[224]</a></sup>.
- **Lean on specific proof.** A credible adoption claim names a customer and an outcome, not a general promise <sup><a href="#sources">[265]</a></sup>.

The early majority buys the whole product, not the core feature, so adoption depends on the support, integration, and services around the capability <sup><a href="#sources">[274]</a></sup>. Product marketing that ships a feature and stops has handed the customer a task, not a habit.

## Run the expansion motion

Expansion is adoption that turned into revenue. The motion is to find the accounts that already reached value and grow the footprint from that proof.

Expansion revenue runs 10 to 30 percent of new MRR for a healthy business, and top performers exceed 60 percent <sup><a href="#sources">[156]</a></sup>. Expansion as a share of new ARR climbed from about 25 percent in 2022 to about 40 percent in 2024, so the motion carries more of the growth plan each year <sup><a href="#sources">[236]</a></sup>. Customer expansion accounts for 52 percent of new revenue in the benchmark set <sup><a href="#sources">[339]</a></sup>.

The expansion play draws on the same proof the launch built:

1. **Find the accounts that reached value.** Usage by cohort, not a guess. The account that hit the activation event is the account ready to grow.
2. **Name the next outcome.** Expansion sells the next result, not more of the same. Seat growth, a new team, and a higher tier each need their own claim.
3. **Price the growth against the value.** The value metric decides what "more" costs. A clean metric makes the upgrade obvious <sup><a href="#sources">[221]</a></sup>. Read [Pricing framework](/docs/foundations/pricing) for the layers.
4. **Keep the proof current.** The reference that earned the first deal earns the expansion only while the outcome still holds <sup><a href="#sources">[265]</a></sup>.

Gross dollar retention in the 85 to 95 percent band is the floor. Net dollar retention of 110 to 120 percent is the target that separates a growing base from a leaking one <sup><a href="#sources">[221]</a></sup>.

## How the job changes by stage

- **Seed.** The founder watches the first ten accounts use the product and writes the activation event from what they see. No adoption tooling yet.
- **First product marketing hire.** One activation event, one adoption motion, and one expansion play per segment. Keep the source of truth in one place.
- **Growth.** Adoption splits into release notes, lifecycle messaging, and in-product guidance, each with an owner.
- **Scale.** Product marketing owns the adoption system and the expansion playbook; enablement owns the artifacts that reach the rep <sup><a href="#sources">[299]</a></sup>. Customer success owns the health signal.

## Failure modes

- **A launch with no adoption plan.** The feature ships and the usage never follows. Fix: name the activation event before the launch, not after.
- **Adoption measured by completion.** A completion rate tells you a user clicked, not that they reached value. Fix: measure the behavior that predicts retention <sup><a href="#sources">[237]</a></sup>.
- **Expansion sold as more seats.** The rep asks for a bigger contract before the customer feels the first win. Fix: sell the next outcome, backed by the proof the account already holds.
- **A proof pack that ages.** The reference case is two years old and the buyer checks. Fix: refresh the proof on a cadence and retire what no longer holds.
- **Adoption work with no owner.** Release notes, tours, and expansion plays drift across teams and the customer sees a different story in each. Fix: name one owner for the adoption system <sup><a href="#sources">[299]</a></sup>.

## Worked example: a launch that sticks

A team launches an analytics feature to mid-market operations leads. The launch narrative lands and the first cohort signs up. Three weeks later, usage has fallen to a third of the peak.

The product marketer pulls the activation data. The customers who stay all connect a second data source in week one; the ones who churn never do. She names that connection the activation event.

She rewrites the release note around the second-source step, adds an in-product prompt at the empty state, and sends a lifecycle email to the cohort that stalled. The day-7 return for the next cohort climbs, and the accounts that connect the second source become the first expansion conversations. The launch earned its first use; the adoption work earned the second.

## Adoption and expansion checklist

```text
ADOPTION AND EXPANSION CHECKLIST
[ ] One named activation event, tested for retention divergence
[ ] Day-7 return read by cohort
[ ] Release note written in the buyer's language
[ ] In-product path to the activation event is one step shorter
[ ] Expansion play names the next outcome, not more seats
[ ] Value metric makes the upgrade obvious
[ ] One proof point per expansion segment, refreshed on a cadence
[ ] One owner for the adoption system
```

## Agentic layer

An agent can watch cohort usage, flag accounts that reached the activation event and stalled before expansion, draft release notes from the launch narrative, and surface a proof point that has not carried a deal in 90 days. A human still approves anything that names a customer, a competitor, or a number. Set the guardrail first: the agent drafts and flags, and never messages a customer without approval. Read [Agentic outbound](/docs/agentic/agentic-outbound) for the desk-level SOP.
