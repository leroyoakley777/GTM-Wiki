# Launch and message testing

A launch is the moment a new product, feature, or position meets the market for the first time. Most launches fail because the team treats them as a press event, not as a reason for a buyer to act. Product marketing owns the launch because it owns the claim the market hears.

The 95-5 rule sets the frame: only about 5% of B2B buyers are in-market at any given time, and the other 95% build memory for a later decision <sup><a href="#sources">[312]</a></sup>. A launch that only talks to this quarter's pipeline leaves the other 95% with no reason to remember you.

This page covers what a launch ships, how to test the message before you scale spend, and the failure modes that turn a launch into a blog post. Pair it with [Product marketing](/docs/product-marketing) for the function, [Positioning and messaging](/docs/foundations/positioning-and-messaging) for the craft, [Win-loss](/docs/foundations/win-loss) for the loss reasons that reprice the message, and [Proof pack and references](/docs/enablement/proof-and-references) for the evidence behind the claim.

## A launch is a market event, not a press release

A launch earns its place when a named buyer segment has a reason to reach out. Before you write a word of copy, answer one question: what changed for the ICP, and why now?

A message lands when the reader recognizes the problem before they hear the product <sup><a href="#sources">[224]</a></sup>. Positioning sets the context that tells a buyer what to compare you against <sup><a href="#sources">[257]</a></sup>. A launch without that context asks the buyer to do the framing work alone.

The launch decision rests on a few inputs:

- **The segment.** Who feels this change today, and who does not.
- **The reason to reach.** The trigger that makes a quiet buyer start looking now.
- **The proof.** The named customer and named outcome that backs the claim.

A feature ships when it works. A launch ships when a segment has a reason to reach.

## Tier the launch to the change

Not every change deserves the same launch. A launch tier spends effort in proportion to how much the market has to change its mind.

| Tier | What changed | Launch motion | Effort |
|------|--------------|---------------|--------|
| Tier 1: category | A new product or a new market position | Full launch: narrative, proof, demand, and sales kit | Weeks of cross-team work |
| Tier 2: segment | A new use case or a new buyer segment | Targeted campaign to the segment with one story | One to two weeks |
| Tier 3: feature | An improvement to an existing product | Enablement note and one sales line | Days |

Match the tier to the change, not to the team's appetite. A Tier 3 feature that gets a Tier 1 launch spends the market's attention on something small.

## Anatomy of a launch narrative

The narrative is the short story the whole launch points at. Keep it to one page a rep can say aloud.

| Field | What it holds | Done looks like |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|
| The change | What is different in the world | "buyers research alone before they talk to a rep" |
| Who it is for | The named segment and buyer role | "mid-market SaaS sales leaders" |
| Why now | The trigger that makes it urgent | A dated market shift |
| The claim | One sentence a buyer can repeat | A single, specific promise |
| The proof | The named customer and outcome | "Ramp, cycle time down 22 percent, as of Q1 2026" |
| The reason to reach | The action the buyer takes next | "book a 20-minute fit call" |

A credible narrative leans on vivid, specific detail and testable proof, not on vague praise <sup><a href="#sources">[265]</a></sup>. The buyer is the hero of the story and the vendor is the guide <sup><a href="#sources">[256]</a></sup>. A narrative that stars the product reads as a feature list.

## Test the message before you scale spend

Outbound is the cheapest message lab a product marketing team can run. Reply quality tells you whether the claim lands before you spend on paid demand.

Run the test in order:

1. **Write the claim in the buyer's language.** Pull the words from closed-won calls, not from the product brief.
2. **Send it as a first line and an ask.** A small, clean send beats a big, noisy one.
3. **Read the replies, not the open rate.** A polite "not now" is signal. A "how did you know?" is a buying trigger.
4. **Reprice the claim from the replies.** Keep the lines that earn meetings and cut the rest.

Reply quality is the lab. If reply rates sit under 2 percent with clean delivery, treat it as a message or audience problem before you treat it as an SDR problem <sup><a href="#sources">[329]</a></sup>. Typical cold reply rates run 1 to 5 percent, and personalization that carries a real signal lifts the reply rate from about 9 percent to 18 percent <sup><a href="#sources">[327]</a></sup>. A message that beats the baseline has earned the next spend.

A differentiated feature is only valuable when a customer can prove it against the real alternative they would otherwise use <sup><a href="#sources">[257]</a></sup>. The message test proves the claim against the market, not against the team's opinion.

## How the launch job differs by stage

- **Seed.** The founder is the product marketer and the first proof. Write the narrative after the tenth sales call, not before the first <sup><a href="#sources">[119]</a></sup>.
- **First PMM hire.** Launch one segment at a time. Keep one owner for the source of truth so three decks do not drift.
- **Growth.** Split launch, competitive, and sales content. A launch ships with a proof pack and a battlecard beside it <sup><a href="#sources">[292]</a></sup>.
- **Scale.** Product marketing owns the launch system and the message library. Marketing owns the demand spend. Enablement owns the sales kit that reaches the rep <sup><a href="#sources">[299]</a></sup>.

## Failure modes

- **Launch as a press event.** A launch with no reason for the ICP to reach is a blog post. Fix: name the segment and the trigger before you write copy.
- **Message by committee.** The claim carries every team's favorite phrase and no buyer's words. Fix: run the outbound test and cut the lines that do not earn a reply.
- **Proof that lags the claim.** The narrative promises an outcome no named customer can show. Fix: attach the proof before the launch, not after <sup><a href="#sources">[224]</a></sup>.
- **The 95% ignored.** The launch only talks to this quarter's in-market buyers. Fix: build memory with a durable narrative for the other 95% <sup><a href="#sources">[312]</a></sup>.
- **Launch then silence.** The team ships the announcement and never tests the message again. Fix: run the outbound lab every quarter and reprice the claim.

## Worked example: a launch that earns its spend

A team ships a new analytics feature. The first instinct is a press release and a webinar. The product marketer runs one test instead.

She writes the claim in the buyer's words: "you can see which accounts are about to churn, this week, without a data team." She sends it to 400 mid-market CS leaders as a first line. Nine percent reply, and four ask for a call. The old claim, "our new analytics suite," drew no replies in the prior send.

She reprices the launch around the churn signal, attaches one customer story with a named outcome, and only then spends on paid demand. The message earned the spend before the budget moved.

## Launch and message checklist

```text
LAUNCH AND MESSAGE CHECKLIST
[ ] One named segment with a reason to reach
[ ] One narrative page a rep can say aloud
[ ] One named customer with a named outcome
[ ] One outbound test before any paid spend
[ ] Launch tier matched to the size of the change
[ ] Enablement kit reaches the rep in the workflow
[ ] Message re-tested every quarter
```

## Agentic layer

An agent can draft first-line variants from the current narrative, run a small send, and flag claims that have not earned a reply in 90 days. It can also watch for a message that drifted from the positioning doc. A human still owns anything that names a customer, a competitor, or a number. Set the guardrail first: the agent drafts and flags, and never sends to a customer without approval. See [Agentic outbound](/docs/agentic/agentic-outbound) for the desk-level SOP.
