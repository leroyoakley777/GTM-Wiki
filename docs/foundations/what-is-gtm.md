# What Is Go-to-Market

A go-to-market strategy is the operational plan that carries a product from
final development to revenue with a named set of customers. It is not a
marketing strategy, a business plan, or a positioning exercise. It binds
together market sizing, the ideal customer profile, positioning, sales
motion, pricing, channel mix, and measurement into one set of decisions that
a cross-functional team can execute week after week.

The value of a GTM strategy appears the moment marketing, product, sales,
and customer success stop working from different playbooks. Without that
shared plan, SDRs call accounts alphabetically, marketing chases generic
intent signals, and AEs select accounts by personal preference. The
ideal customer profile looks correct on paper, but the pipeline stalls and
internal friction rises. The strategy was not wrong. The coordination was
missing.

Companies with a structured GTM strategy close deals faster and spend less
per acquisition than companies without one. A go-to-market strategy reduces
risk by validating demand, testing messaging, and setting clear success
metrics before a full-scale launch. The cost of skipping that step appears as
wasted ad spend, stalled pipeline, and churned first customers.

## GTM strategy versus the documents people confuse it with

Three documents circulate in most startups, and they are not the same thing.
A business plan defines the company's overall model and financial outlook
over years. A marketing strategy creates demand and reaches buyers within an
existing category. A GTM strategy brings a specific product to a specific
market with a specific sales motion. Marketing strategy lives inside GTM
strategy. GTM strategy lives inside the business plan.

| Element | GTM strategy | Marketing strategy | Business plan |
|---------|-------------|--------------------|---------------|
| Purpose | Bring a specific product to a defined market | Create demand and reach buyers | Define the business model and financial outlook |
| Horizon | Launch through early traction, 3 to 18 months | Ongoing, campaign-cycle driven | 3 to 5 years |
| Owner | GTM leadership, RevOps, PMM, product | Marketing, PMM | Executive team, finance, board |
| Timing | Before product launch or market entry | Continuously updated | At founding or major inflection |
| Output | ICP, positioning, sales motion, pricing, KPIs | Campaign plans, channel mix, messaging | P&L, market sizing, funding path |
| Metric | Time to revenue, win rate, CAC payback | MQL volume, CPL, pipeline share | Revenue growth, unit economics |

Marketing can own campaigns and channels. Only a cross-functional GTM
plan owns how those campaigns connect to revenue.

## Core frameworks

Three frameworks structure every GTM effort. Choose the one that fits your
stage and motion, then extend it with the ten elements below.

### Ten-element working checklist

Every GTM strategy must answer ten questions. This list adapts the eight GTM
Operating System pillars from GTM Partners into a working checklist teams
can ship against [181].

1. Market sizing (TAM, SAM, SOM, PAM). Tier your opportunity so energy
   flows to the most realistic and valuable slice.
2. Target customer. Define the firmographic, technographic, and behavioral
   profile that wins fastest. See [ICP Definition](/docs/foundations/icp-definition).
3. Value proposition. Translate product features into outcomes the target
   actually cares about.
4. Product positioning. Establish how the solution fits relative to
   alternatives so the value is clear. See
   [Positioning and Messaging](/docs/foundations/positioning-and-messaging).
5. Sales strategy. Outline the sales process, channels, and org structure
   needed to drive revenue.
6. Marketing strategy. Demand generation, content, digital, and brand
   tactics aligned to the broader GTM plan.
7. Customer journey and experience. Map how buyers move through each phase,
   including backward movement, and align messaging to each stage.
8. Distribution channels. Direct sales, self-serve, resellers, or
   marketplaces. Mitigate channel conflict up front.
9. Core metrics and KPIs. Pipeline growth, conversion rates, CAC, LTV, win
   rate, NRR. Measure all four buckets.
10. Launch and enablement. Coordinate internal teams and equip sales and
    support with training, tools, and content.

### Execution models

A GTM effort can run as a funnel, a flywheel, a product-led motion, or an
account-based motion. Most mature strategies combine product-led growth for
self-serve with account-based targeting for high-ACV accounts. The motion
you choose determines org shape, hiring, metrics, and cadence.

| Model | Best fit | Ownership |
|-------|----------|-----------|
| Classic funnel | Enterprise, complex sales | Marketing top of funnel, sales bottom |
| Flywheel | Subscription, retention-heavy | Customer success at the center |
| Product-led | Low ACV, fast value | Product and growth |
| Account-based | High ACV, few targets | Sales and marketing on shared account list |

### GTM motions by ACV and sales cycle

| Dimension | Product-led | Sales-led | Hybrid |
|-----------|------------|-----------|--------|
| ICP signal | Usage threshold, product-qualified lead | Firmographics and intent data | Both |
| Primary KPI | Activation rate, NRR | Pipeline volume, win rate | CAC payback, NRR |
| Pricing | Self-serve tiers | Custom or usage-based | Tiered plus sales assist |
| Sales cycle | Days to weeks | Weeks to months | Weeks |
| Org shape | Product and CS | SDR, AE, SE | PLG plus sales overlay |

## Seven-step build sequence

A working GTM plan follows a single build sequence. Each step produces a
deliverable that the next step depends on. Skip a step and the plan develops
gaps that compound later.

1. Define the ICP from at least 20 customer conversations. Do not guess.
   Talk to customers and document the firmographics, technographics, pain
   points, and triggers that mark your best-fit accounts [69].
2. Build positioning anchored in pain points, not features. The positioning
   statement must answer a clear question: for the ICP, who faces a specific
   pain, the product is a category that delivers a benefit. Unlike the
   alternative, the product wins because of a defensible difference [265].
3. Choose the GTM motion. Product-led, sales-led, channel, or
   community-led. The motion dictates org structure, hiring, metrics, and
   cadence. See [Channel Selection](/docs/foundations/gtm-channels-bullseye).
4. Define the pricing model. Seat-based, usage, tiered, or hybrid. Pricing
   must align to the GTM motion and the unit economics of the motion. See
   [Pricing as GTM](/docs/data/pricing-as-gtm).
5. Set KPIs in four buckets. Leading, lagging, quality, and efficiency.
   Every team owns a number that rolls up to revenue. See
   [GTM Metrics](/docs/foundations/gtm-metrics).
6. Align cross-functional teams on shared signals. One source of truth so
   marketing, sales, and CS act on the same buying signals and intent data.
7. Design a feedback loop for monthly revision. Quarterly cycles surface
   problems too late for modern B2B markets. The strongest GTM strategies
   review monthly.

```text
GTM STRATEGY CHECKLIST
1. ICP               Firmographics, technographics, pain points, buying triggers
2. PAM               Top 10 percent of TAM showing in-market intent
3. Value prop        For [ICP], who [pain], our product is a [category] that [benefit]
4. Positioning       Category plus differentiation versus the named alternative
5. GTM motion        Product-led | Sales-led | Hybrid | Channel | Community
6. Pricing           Seat | Usage | Tiered | Hybrid. ACV: ___. Payback: ___ months.
7. Channels          Primary ___. Secondary ___. (From the bullseye.)
8. KPIs              Pipeline ___. Conversion ___. CAC payback ___. NRR: ___.
9. Owners            RACI per motion: R=___, A=___, C=___, I=___.
10. Cadence          Monthly: W1 pipeline, W2 win-loss, W3 signals, W4 tune.
```

## GTM by company stage and scale

The framework stays the same. The emphasis shifts by company stage and by
the GTM motion selected.

| Stage | Focus |
|-------|-------|
| Pre-PMF | ICP discovery, value proposition, one channel |
| $0 to $1M ARR | Motion selection, pricing, CAC payback |
| $1M to $10M ARR | Repeatable motion, compensation design, forecasting |
| $10M to $100M ARR | Phase shifts, data model, expansion motion |

## Common mistakes

### Treating GTM as a one-time exercise

The strongest GTM strategies are repeatable and adaptive. They keep teams
aligned, provide a shared framework, and evolve as customers and market
conditions change. Review monthly, not quarterly.

### Confusing tactics with strategy

Tactics are specific motions that teams execute: an outreach sequence, a
campaign budget, a discounting threshold. Strategy gives structure so
tactics stay coordinated instead of running randomly.

### No unified framework connecting teams

A solid ICP on paper breaks when SDRs, marketing, and AEs operate on
different logic. The result is stalled pipeline and internal friction.
The strategy was correct. The coordination failed.

### Targeting broadly instead of narrowly

Narrow focus accelerates initial traction. A precisely defined ICP lets the
team concentrate resources, create reference customers, and expand from a
position of strength. Broad targeting dilutes messaging and wastes CAC.

### Ignoring the feedback loop

A GTM strategy is not a static document. It is an evolving roadmap. The most
successful organizations maintain strategic direction while incorporating
continuous learning from implementation. Without a designed review process,
the strategy calcifies while the market moves.

## B2B versus B2C GTM differences

| Element | B2B | B2C |
|---------|-----|-----|
| Sales cycle | Weeks to months, 3 to 12 plus for enterprise | Days to weeks, often impulse |
| Decision-makers | 6 to 10 stakeholders, buying committee | One individual |
| Primary channels | Direct sales, ABM, SDR outreach, events, content | Paid social, SEO, influencer, retail, e-commerce |
| Pricing | Usage, seat, tiered, annual contracts | Flat-rate, subscription, one-time purchase |
| Success metrics | Pipeline, ARR, CAC payback, NRR, win rate | Conversion rate, CAC, LTV, ROAS |

For high-ACV B2B deals, account-based motion is the dominant approach. It
concentrates resources on accounts most likely to close and coordinates
sales and marketing around a shared account list.
