# Win-Loss and Customer Research

A win-loss program turns closed deals into evidence. It answers four questions: why buyers choose you, why they leave, how those reasons change, and what the team does next <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>. Customer research runs the same loop on open deals and renewals, while the buyer still weighs the options.

This work sits between [ICP Definition](./icp-definition) and [Positioning and Messaging](./positioning-and-messaging). The ICP and the positioning claim to know the buyer. Research tests the claim against the people who decided. When the two disagree, the research wins.

## Definition

Win-loss analysis is the systematic study of closed deals, won and lost, through interviews with the people who made the decision. Customer research applies the same method to open opportunities, renewals, and churned accounts. Both replace internal opinion with buyer evidence.

Three terms sit in one system and are not interchangeable:

- **Win-loss analysis** studies the deal after the outcome.
- **Customer research** studies the buyer before and during the evaluation.
- **Loss taxonomy** is the coded classification that turns single interviews into a pattern.

Drop the taxonomy and every interview becomes an anecdote. Drop the interviews and the taxonomy runs on fields a rep fills in from memory. Run all three and the findings reprice the ICP, the positioning, and the discovery script.

## Why buyer evidence beats internal opinion

Most teams trust the CRM. A rep codes a deal "lost on price," and the field becomes the reason. Stated reasons rarely match real causes <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>. An analysis of 7.2 million recorded B2B buyer conversations found that 58% of late-stage deals fail on implementation risk, not price <sup><a href="#sources">[294]</a></sup>. The same study found that underperforming on the buyer's top priority cuts win rates by up to 10 percentage points <sup><a href="#sources">[294]</a></sup>. When the loss code says price and the recording says rollout fear, the team fixes the wrong thing and loses the same deal again.

Buyers do most of the work before a rep enters. 86% of B2B purchases stall, and 81% of buyers report dissatisfaction with the process <sup><a href="#sources">[43]</a></sup>. About 13 people join the buying decision, and 89% of decisions span two or more departments <sup><a href="#sources">[43]</a></sup>. A team that never interviews the buyer guesses at a committee it has never met.

Win rate is the number this work moves. Average B2B win rate runs near 21%, and the opportunity-to-closed-won rate has fallen 4 to 6 points since 2021 <sup><a href="#sources">[144]</a></sup>. A defined ICP tracks with 68% higher win rates <sup><a href="#sources">[30]</a></sup>. Early involvement of the decision maker lifts win rates by 55%, and a deal that slips carries a win rate 113% lower than a deal that stays on schedule <sup><a href="#sources">[339]</a></sup>. Each number is a research question, not a trophy for the dashboard.

## Interview loop

Interview the buyer, not the rep. The rep knows what the rep saw. The buyer knows what the committee weighed. Most programs interview only the reps and call it win-loss.

Follow the conversation rules that make any customer interview honest <sup><a href="#sources">[250]</a></sup>:

- Talk about their life, not your product.
- Ask about past behavior, not future intent.
- Let the customer speak 80% or more of the time.
- Treat commitment as the only reliable signal. Words are cheap.

People lie to be nice, so a polite "we loved the demo" proves nothing <sup><a href="#sources">[250]</a></sup>. Past behavior beats a prediction every time. Ask what the buyer did, not what the buyer plans to do.

Interview within two weeks of the decision, while memory is fresh. For a loss, interview the buyer who said no and the champion who tried to say yes. For a win, interview the buyer to learn which proof point closed the deal. For a churned account, interview the user who left.

A short script keeps the interviews comparable. Adjust the wording, keep the shape.

```text
WIN-LOSS INTERVIEW SCRIPT
1. Walk me through how you made this decision.
2. What did you do before you started looking? (workaround, cost)
3. Who else weighed in, and what did each person care about?
4. What were you comparing us against?
5. What almost stopped the decision?
6. What did we get wrong?
7. If you chose someone else, what did they promise that we did not?
```

## Loss taxonomy

A taxonomy turns interviews into a pattern. Code every closed deal, won and lost, against the same list. Sampling introduces selection bias and loses signal, so capture the full set <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>. The win-rate base must also be large enough to read. A useful base holds at least 100 qualifying closed deals <sup><a href="#sources">[342]</a></sup>.

| Code | What it means | What to change |
|---|---|---|
| No decision | The buyer did nothing | Build urgency, cut the effort to switch |
| Implementation risk | Fear of a failed rollout | Sell the migration path, name the onboarding |
| Missing capability | A required feature absent | Feed the roadmap, requalify the ICP |
| Price | Budget or perceived value gap | Test the value metric, recheck packaging |
| Wrong buyer | The decision maker never engaged | Fix qualification and the entry point |
| Champion loss | The internal advocate left or lost | Multi-thread earlier, coach the champion |
| Competitor | A named rival won | Update the battlecard and discovery |
| Timing | The need moved out | Set a trigger-based follow-up |

Triangulate each significant loss across three inputs: the rep report, the buyer interview, and the call data <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>. When the three agree, the reason is probably accurate. When they diverge, the gap is the insight.

A low win rate with no competitor named points to a different problem. When buyers do not mention a rival and still stall, the team has an urgency gap, not a competitive one <sup><a href="#sources">[342]</a></sup>. Recode those deals as No decision and work the urgency, not the battlecard.

## Reprice the ICP and the positioning

Research earns its cost only when it changes a decision. Two decisions matter most: who the ICP includes, and what the positioning claims.

Reprice the ICP when the interviews show a different buyer than the one on the page. If the daily sufferer sits below the assumed economic buyer, the ICP is wrong, not the rep. If a segment never mentions the trigger the ICP assumes, drop it and narrow.

Reprice the positioning when the interviews show a gap between what you say and what the buyer heard. Weak positioning shows up as prospects who cannot say what you sell, long cycles with low conversion, and churn from wrong expectations <sup><a href="#sources">[257]</a></sup>. Each sign is a finding the interviews can name.

Feed the findings into the artifacts reps use, on a monthly cadence: the battlecard, the discovery questions, and the objection responses <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>. A finding that stays in a research folder changes nothing. See [Competitive Analysis](./competitive-analysis) for the battlecard anatomy, and [Pricing Framework](./pricing) when the loss code reads "price."

### Research depth by stage

The program changes shape as the company grows.

**Seed.** Talk to every closed deal. The base is small, so each interview carries weight. Write the first loss taxonomy from the first ten decisions.

**Early traction.** Interview every loss and a sample of wins. Split findings by segment to see whether the ICP holds across the beachhead.

**Growth.** Code every deal in the CRM and interview on a monthly cadence. Watch the reasons change over time, because a reason that shifts is a market that moves.

**Scale.** Automate the coding, run the interviews by segment, and review the taxonomy in the weekly pipeline meeting. At scale, the loop must live in the operating rhythm, not a quarterly report <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>.

## Failure modes and fixes

- **Sampling the losses.** Picking ten deals to study invites selection bias and drops the signal. Fix: code every closed deal and interview the significant ones <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>.
- **Trusting the CRM reason.** "Lost on price" often hides a rollout fear. Fix: triangulate the rep report, the buyer interview, and the call data before you believe the code <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>.
- **Interviewing the rep, not the buyer.** The rep reports what the rep saw. Fix: interview the buyer who decided, plus the champion.
- **No rep-facing output.** Research that stays in a folder changes no behavior. Fix: push every finding into the battlecard, the discovery script, or the objection list each month <sup><a href="#sources">[293]</a></sup>.
- **No taxonomy.** Without codes, every interview is a story and no pattern forms. Fix: code against one list, and recode when the list stops fitting.
- **Recency bias.** The last loud loss crowds out the pattern. Fix: read the codes over a full quarter, not the last conversation.

## Research review checklist

```text
WIN-LOSS PROGRAM CHECKLIST
[ ] Every closed deal coded against one taxonomy
[ ] Buyer interviewed for each significant win and loss
[ ] Champion and decision maker both heard from
[ ] Rep report, buyer interview, and call data triangulated
[ ] Reasons compared over time, not just this quarter
[ ] Findings pushed into battlecards and discovery monthly
[ ] ICP updated when the buyer differs from the page
[ ] Positioning updated when the buyer misread the claim
[ ] Base of at least 100 closed deals before you trust the rate
```

## Run it today

One. Pull the last 50 closed deals. Code each one against the taxonomy above. Count the codes. The top two codes name your real GTM problem, whatever the kickoff deck says.

Two. Interview five buyers, three losses and two wins, within two weeks. Ask the seven questions. Write down the exact words, because the words become the new positioning.

Three. Take the top loss code and change one artifact this week. If the code is Implementation risk, add a migration question to discovery. If the code is No decision, add an urgency step to the sales process. Ship the change, then re-read the codes next month.
