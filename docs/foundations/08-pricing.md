# Pricing Framework

Pricing is a go-to-market decision, not a finance formality. The price, the metric it attaches to, and the packaging around it decide who buys, how fast they buy, and how long they stay. This page is the foundations treatment of the system. The benchmark numbers live in [Pricing as a GTM Lever](/docs/data/pricing-as-gtm).

## Definition

Pricing names the exchange rate between the value a customer receives and the money they send you. A pricing framework turns that rate into a system with three layers: the value metric, the packaging, and the price point. Each layer answers a different question, and each one can break the model on its own.

## Why pricing decides the motion

Pricing sets the shape of the whole GTM motion. A self-serve motion lives or dies on a simple, low-friction price. A sales-led motion needs room to negotiate and a defensible rate card. When price and motion disagree, the motion loses: reps discount to close, self-serve conversion stalls, and CAC payback stretches.

Pricing also signals value. A clean, consistent price tells buyers the product is worth the ask. A patchwork of one-off discounts tells them the list price was a story. Buyers compare notes across accounts and across conferences, so pricing inconsistency erodes trust across the base, not just inside one deal.

## Layer one: the value metric

The value metric is the unit the price attaches to. It must grow as the customer's value grows.

- Seats fit products where value scales with people.
- Usage fits products where value scales with consumption: messages, records, compute, data volume.
- Outcomes fit products where value ties to a measured business result.

The metric is the realization ceiling. A wrong metric caps revenue no matter how disciplined the discounting. When agents let one team handle far more volume at the same headcount, a per-seat metric charges the same for the extra output. Consumption and outcome metrics fix that by attaching price to output instead of headcount <sup><a href="#sources">[270]</a></sup>.

## Layer two: packaging

Packaging decides which capabilities sit together and who pays for them. It moves more revenue than any single price point.

Build packaging around customer groups, not market segments. A customer group is a cluster of buyers who derive value in similar ways. Groups rarely line up with industry codes or firmographic buckets. When packaging follows demographics instead of value, buyers who need an odd combination of features fall between tiers, and reps paper over the gap with hidden discounts. Two customers then pay different prices for the same capabilities.

Good-better-best is the dominant model: 40.8% of B2B SaaS companies use it as their primary tiering <sup><a href="#sources">[89]</a></sup>. It works because it matches how buyers compare options, with a low-friction entry tier, a mainstream tier, and an enterprise tier that carries governance and support.

### Pricing by motion

| Motion | Value metric | Main tension |
|--------|-------------|-------------|
| Product-led | Usage with a self-serve ceiling | Must stay simple at low volume |
| Sales-led | Seat or usage tiers | Needs guardrails against over-counting |
| Consumption | Pay per event or output | Harder for the buyer to forecast |
| Outcome | Pay per measured result | Attribution must be provable |

## Layer three: the price point

A price point must reflect validated willingness to pay. Surveys rarely give that answer. B2B value sits in complex usage patterns, several stakeholders decide, and buyers cannot price an operational improvement they have not experienced yet.

The empirical method is revealed preference. Run small controlled price changes against cohorts with similar usage and value characteristics, then read what the cohort does, not what it says. This keeps price in step with the value the roadmap creates. A price cut spends the same effort and gives back margin for no gain. The sequence matters more than the direction.

## Pricing by stage and motion

The model that fits a first customer breaks at scale, and the discipline that fits scale is overkill at the start. Match the system to the stage you are in, not the stage you are moving toward.

**Seed.** Keep the model simple and change it often. You have no expansion base and no discount history. Charge for the metric you believe in, prove it on the first closed-won accounts, and treat the list price as a hypothesis <sup><a href="#sources">[270]</a></sup><sup><a href="#sources">[89]</a></sup>.

**Early traction.** Discount discipline starts to matter once deals repeat. Keep the 5-20% band and require a trade for anything above it. Pick one packaging model, usually good-better-best, and hold it <sup><a href="#sources">[104]</a></sup><sup><a href="#sources">[89]</a></sup>.

**Growth.** Add the expansion path before the first contract signs. Healthy expansion revenue runs 10-30% of new revenue, and top performers clear 60% of new MRR from expansion <sup><a href="#sources">[212]</a></sup>. Publish a rate card so renewals do not restart from zero <sup><a href="#sources">[104]</a></sup><sup><a href="#sources">[270]</a></sup>.

**Scale.** The system fragments by segment. A self-serve motion anchors near the $702 CAC floor and a sales-led motion carries the $11,400 ceiling <sup><a href="#sources">[209]</a></sup>. One model cannot serve both ends of that spread. Tie discount authority to margin, not quota date <sup><a href="#sources">[270]</a></sup><sup><a href="#sources">[104]</a></sup>.

## Discount discipline

A discount for behavior you want is pricing. Annual prepay at 15-20% off rewards a commitment that lowers churn and speeds cash <sup><a href="#sources">[104]</a></sup>.

A discount conceded to close is a subsidy. It buys the deal and caps the customer's lifetime value, because heavy-discount customers carry higher churn. The optimal band sits at 5-20%. Past 40% the discount stops winning marginal deals and starts shrinking the ones you already hold <sup><a href="#sources">[104]</a></sup>.

Compensation is the hidden driver. Research on enterprise software deals found 74% closed on the last day of the quarter, with late-quarter discounts averaging 35-37% against 30% mid-quarter <sup><a href="#sources">[104]</a></sup>. That gap tracks reps pulling deals forward to hit a threshold. A comp plan that pays volume over margin erodes pricing no matter how clean the model is.

## Failure modes and fixes

- **Discounting to close.** A subsidy caps lifetime value and becomes the floor for the next deal. Fix: hold the 5-20% band, require a trade above it, and route quotes over 20% off to a deal desk <sup><a href="#sources">[104]</a></sup>.
- **Wrong value metric.** Seats bill headcount while agents raise output. Fix: attach price to usage or outcome and prove the metric on closed-won data before you rewrite the list <sup><a href="#sources">[270]</a></sup>.
- **Template packaging.** Editions copied from a competitor fit the average buyer and no group. Fix: cluster buyers by how they derive value, then sell the capability only one group wants.
- **No expansion path.** A model with nowhere to grow caps NRR near the median of 102%. Fix: place the next tier one cap-break away before the first contract signs <sup><a href="#sources">[212]</a></sup>.
- **Comp that pays volume.** Late-quarter discounts of 35-37% are a comp problem, not a list-price problem. Fix: tie discount authority to margin <sup><a href="#sources">[104]</a></sup>.
- **Survey-based price.** Stated preference misses value the buyer has not felt. Fix: set list from revealed preference on matched cohorts, then move in small steps.

## Pricing review checklist

```
PRICING SYSTEM CHECKLIST
[ ] Value metric: usage or outcome, not seats
[ ] Metric grows with the value the customer receives
[ ] Customer groups identified from usage and renewal data
[ ] Packaging built on groups, not demographics
[ ] Good-better-best or consumption tiers held steady
[ ] Annual prepay discount 15-20%
[ ] Discount guardrail: 5-20% band, quotes over 20% flagged
[ ] Expansion path live before the first contract
[ ] Discount authority tied to margin, not quota date
[ ] Rate card published for renewals
```

## Run it today

One. Pull the last 50 closed-won contracts. Group them by usage volume and deal size, not by industry, and find which usage traits track renewal.

Two. Flag every deal that closed over 20% off. Trace each one back to the rep's quota date. If the discounts cluster near quarter close, the problem sits in the comp plan, not the list price.

Three. Take the capability your largest customers use and your smallest customers ignore. Sell it as an add-on to the group that values it, and ship the change at the next renewal.
