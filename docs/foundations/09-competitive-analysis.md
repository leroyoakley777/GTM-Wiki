# Competitive Analysis

Competitive analysis answers two questions. Is this market structurally winnable, and who actually takes the deals we want? Most teams only ask the second one, and most answer it wrong.

They focus on the biggest, best-funded name in the category and ignore the alternatives they actually lose to. Then they build a comparison matrix once, present it at kickoff, and never update it. Six months later the matrix is stale, sales has stopped using it, and competitive win rates still are not moving.

This page covers both lenses. The structural lens, Porter's five forces, tells you whether the industry lets anyone make money. The deal lens tells you who you really compete with, including the buyer who does nothing. Then it covers the system that turns raw intelligence into tools a rep can use on a live call.

---

## Definition

Competitive analysis is the systematic process of understanding how the alternatives in your market position themselves, what they offer, how they sell, and where they are vulnerable. [1]

It is a process, not a deliverable. A comparison matrix is an output of one point in time. Analysis is a continuous operation that takes in intelligence, makes sense of it, and pushes out positioning updates, battlecards, and talk tracks.

Three terms sit in one system and are not interchangeable:

- **Competitive intelligence** is the raw input. Facts about competitors: product changes, pricing, hiring, funding, reviews, sales tactics, and marketing messages. Intelligence is observational. You collect it. You do not decide anything from a single data point.
- **Competitive analysis** is the processing layer. It turns raw intelligence into structured judgment: what is this competitor's strategy, where are they investing, where are they strong, where are they weak, what is their likely next move. Analysis requires judgment, so it needs a repeatable structure to stay consistent. [1]
- **Competitive positioning** is the output. It is the strategic decisions you make from the analysis: which battles to fight, which to avoid, and how you train the team to handle competitive deals. Positioning is the competitive layer of your overall positioning architecture, not a separate document. [1]

Skip the bottom layer and your analysis runs on assumptions. Skip the middle layer and your positioning runs on gut feel. Skip the top layer and your analysis is interesting but useless.

## Why competitive analysis matters

Industry structure, not product quality alone, decides who earns durable profit. Two companies with identical products and capabilities produce very different results in industries with different structures. Analysis lets you see that before you commit resources. [2]

The discipline serves three concrete purposes.

First, it picks your market. A five-forces analysis tells you which industries are structurally winnable and which will grind down every player, no matter how good the product. [2] A startup that ignores structure signs up for a fight it cannot win at any price.

Second, it grounds your positioning. Positioning only works against the alternatives the buyer actually has. [3] You cannot differentiate against a competitor you will not admit exists, and you cannot differentiate against nothing.

Third, it wins the deal. Every competitive loss is a data point about how buyers weigh you against the alternatives. A team that learns from every closed deal compounds that learning into better discovery, better battlecards, and a higher win rate. A team that does not keeps losing the same deals for the same reasons.

## Lens one: market structure, the five forces

Michael Porter first published the five forces in 1979. The framework describes the structural forces that decide how economic value in an industry is divided, and therefore whether any player in the industry can sustain profitability. [2]

The five forces:

| Force | What it is | When the force is strong | Strategic response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rivalry among existing competitors | Direct competition between current players | Many competitors of similar size, slow growth, high fixed costs, high exit barriers, committed rivals | Find a niche, differentiate, or win on cost leadership; avoid head-to-head commodity fights |
| Threat of new entrants | New capacity and price pressure from newcomers | Low barriers to entry, well-resourced likely entrants nearby | Build a defensible moat: scale, brand, distribution, or switching costs |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | Suppliers capturing margin by raising prices or tightening terms | One or two suppliers of an essential input, costly switching | Reduce dependence, standardize inputs, build alternatives |
| Bargaining power of buyers | Customers forcing prices down or demanding more service | Few large buyers, undifferentiated products, low switching cost | Differentiate, deepen switching costs, serve segments buyers cannot play against each other |
| Threat of substitutes | A different product meeting the same underlying need | Substitute offers better price-performance and low switching cost | Compete on the underlying job done, not the feature list |

Entry brings new capacity and puts pressure on prices and costs. The threat of entry therefore caps the profit potential of an industry. [2] Rivalry that is intense drives down prices or dissipates profit by raising the cost of competing. [2]

Substitutes matter more than most teams admit. A substitute is another product that meets the same underlying need in a different way. Videoconferencing is a substitute for travel. Email is a substitute for express mail. [2] In software, the spreadsheet and the manual process are the most common substitutes, and they are nearly always ignored.

Two structural points to hold onto. First, industry structure and your relative position within it are the two basic drivers of company profitability. [2] You need both. Second, structure is not static. Buyers and suppliers gain or lose power. Innovation changes entry and substitution. Regulation changes rivalry. A five-forces analysis is a snapshot you revisit, not a fact you file. [2]

The five-forces framework is the tool for the market question. It is the wrong tool for the daily deal question. For that you move to the unit of competition.

## Lens two: the real alternatives

The deal-level question is who you actually compete with. Most teams answer it with the competitor they are afraid of, which is usually the largest player in the category. The buyers they actually lose to are often different. [1]

Start with the alternatives the buyer could choose if you did not exist. April Dunford calls these competitive alternatives, and they set the minimum benchmark you must clear. [3] They are often not other products. They are an internal team, a spreadsheet, an outsourced provider, or doing nothing. The buyer who does nothing is a competitor, and often the hardest one, because no-decision beats you without a rival to blame.

Your real competitive set comes from three data sources, not from opinion: [1]

1. **CRM data.** Pull every closed-lost deal from the last six months and tag the competitor that won. Rank competitors by deal volume lost. This is your competitive set, not the list in the CEO's head.
2. **Sales interviews.** Ask your top five reps who they see most often in deals. Reps surface competitors that never show up in CRM, especially the status quo and manual workarounds.
3. **Buyer research.** In customer interviews, ask what else they evaluated before choosing you. Buyers name alternatives you never considered, including adjacent category players that solve the problem differently.

One point of honesty here. A differentiated feature is only valuable if it is provably valuable to the customer's goal, valuable to a defined segment, and meaningful against the real alternatives the customer cares about. [3] A feature that beats a competitor you barely face is a distraction, not differentiation.

## Run it as a system, not a project

Competitive analysis works as a system with three layers and a cadence. Treat it that way and it compounds. Treat it as a project and it dies.

| Layer | What it is | Cadence | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intelligence | Raw data collection from public, sales, and buyer sources | Ongoing, weekly scans | A consistent feed of facts |
| Analysis | Structured synthesis into strong, weak, win, lose | Monthly for Tier 1 | Updated competitive profiles |
| Positioning | Strategic decisions and rep-facing tools | Quarterly review | Battlecards, talk tracks, positioning updates |

Each layer feeds the next. Intelligence without analysis is noise. Analysis without positioning is trivia. Positioning without fresh intelligence decays.

### Tier your competitive set

Depth beats breadth. A thorough analysis of three competitors beats a shallow scan of fifteen. [1] Rank the set you identified into tiers and match effort to frequency.

| Tier | Definition | Cadence | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | Appears in 20%+ of deals | Deep analysis monthly, dedicated battlecards | Two to four competitors |
| Tier 2 | Appears in 5 to 20% of deals | Standard analysis quarterly, summary battlecards | Three to six competitors |
| Tier 3 | Emerging, adjacent, or occasional | Monitoring only, semi-annual review | Variable count |

Re-rank the tiers every quarter. A Tier 2 competitor that appears in 25% of your deals moves up. A Tier 1 that you no longer see drops down. The tiering is a discipline, not a one-time exercise.

## How to execute

Run the full cycle monthly for your Tier 1 competitors and quarterly for the extended set. [1] The five steps:

### Step 1: Define the competitive set

Pull closed-lost data from the last six months. Interview your top reps. Ask buyers what else they evaluated. Rank who you actually lose to and tier them as above. Resist the urge to analyze every competitor in the category. Focus on the three that take your deals.

### Step 2: Gather intelligence on a cadence

Intelligence must be systematic, not reactive. Do not wait for a lost deal to panic and research.

- **Public sources, weekly.** Competitor product pages, pricing pages, blog, review sites, LinkedIn, press releases, job postings. Job postings reveal investment priorities. Pricing pages reveal packaging strategy.
- **Sales feedback, ongoing.** Give reps a one-field intake: competitor name, deal stage, what they claimed, the outcome. A Slack channel or a form works. Volume matters. Patterns appear at thirty data points, not three. [1]
- **Buyer research, monthly.** Win-loss interviews are your best intelligence source. Ask what impressed the buyer about the competitor, what concerned them, and at what point they eliminated it.
- **Product trials, quarterly.** Sign up for competitor trials. Use the product. Document onboarding, core workflows, and limitations. First-hand experience beats any analyst report. [1]

### Step 3: Analyze into four statements

Raw intelligence becomes usable when you force it into structure. For each competitor, start with their positioning: what category they claim, who they say they are built for. Map their product against yours. Analyze their go-to-market: how they sell, what segments they target, how they price. Then synthesize into four honest statements: [1]

1. **Where they are strong.** The areas where they genuinely have an advantage. Be honest. Pretending competitors have no strengths destroys your credibility with your own reps.
2. **Where they are weak.** Weaknesses that matter to your target buyers, not any weakness you can find.
3. **Where you win.** The scenarios and segments where you consistently beat them, backed by win-loss data.
4. **Where you lose.** The scenarios where they beat you. Knowing this lets reps qualify deals and avoid wasting cycles on unwinnable ones.

### Step 4: Validate with win-loss analysis

Analysis based on assumption decays. Validation comes from the deals that actually closed. Run win-loss analysis as a continuous program, not a quarterly report. [4]

The four questions: Why do we win? Why do we lose? How are those reasons changing? What should the team do differently? [4]

Two practices keep it honest:

- **Capture every deal, not a sample.** The moment you pick ten deals to analyze, you introduce selection bias and lose signal. Code structured data on every closed deal. [4]
- **Triangulate the loss reason.** For every significant loss, gather three inputs: what the rep said, what the buyer said, and what the call data shows. If they agree, the reason is probably accurate. If they diverge, the gap itself is the insight. [4]

Stated reasons are unreliable. A rep logs "lost on price" in the CRM. The call recording shows the buyer worried about a failed rollout. These are not the same problem and they do not have the same fix. Analysis of 7.2 million recorded B2B buyer conversations found that 58% of late-stage deals fail because of implementation risk, not price. [5] Code what actually happened, not what the CRM field says.

### Step 5: Convert analysis into rep-facing tools

Analysis only matters when it changes the next call. Feed findings into three artifacts every month: battlecards, discovery questions, and objection frameworks. [4]

A good battlecard fits on one page and contains five sections: [1]

1. **Quick summary.** Who this competitor is and the one thing your reps need to know. Scannable in ten seconds.
2. **Where we win.** Three to four specific scenarios with proof points. "We win on enterprise scalability. Acme switched from them because they could not handle more than 10,000 concurrent users."
3. **Where they win.** Two to three areas where they genuinely lead. Reps who know their own vulnerability perform better than reps surprised by it on a live call.
4. **Landmine questions.** Three to five discovery questions that expose the competitor's weakness, framed as genuine questions, not attacks. "How are you thinking about scale as you grow?" beats "Did you know they cannot scale?"
5. **Objection responses.** The most common pushback and the specific response, using an acknowledge, bridge, counter pattern.

Build the first draft, then test it with your top reps in their next three competitive deals. The first version is never right. Expect two to three iterations before a battlecard is useful in live selling. [1]

## Ownership and cadence

Clear ownership keeps the program from becoming everyone's job and no one's job. [1]

- **Solo product marketer.** Own everything. Focus on Tier 1 only. Two competitors analyzed deeply beat eight analyzed shallowly.
- **Product marketing team of two to four.** Assign one competitor owner per person. One person owns the overall cadence and the quarterly review.
- **Product marketing team of five or more.** Designate a competitive intelligence lead who owns the program, the collection infrastructure, and distribution. Individual owners cover competitors by segment or product alignment.

In every case, sales is an input source, not an owner. Make reporting easy with a Slack channel or a form. Close the loop: when a rep's intelligence produces a battlecard update, tell them. Sales contributes more intelligence when they see it become a tool they use. [1]

Set the cadence and stick to it. Monthly updates for Tier 1, quarterly review of the whole position, and a standing item in weekly pipeline review where recent losses get discussed. [4]

## A worked example

A workflow-automation vendor sells to mid-market operations leaders. The leadership team believed the only competitor worth tracking was a large enterprise suite everyone feared.

The closed-lost data told a different story. In the last six months the vendor lost twice as many deals to no-decision as to the suite. It lost the rest to a cheaper point tool and to buyers who said they would keep doing the work by hand.

The vendor re-tiered. The enterprise suite stayed Tier 1 because it appeared in a quarter of deals. No-decision became a named Tier 1 competitor, not an excuse. The cheaper point tool dropped to Tier 2.

Win-loss interviews showed the real pattern. Buyers who chose the suite valued integration with their existing stack. Buyers who chose nothing said the cost of switching their team's workflow was too high, a fear of failed rollout, not a price objection. The vendor had been losing on implementation risk and coding the losses as price.

The response was concrete. Battlecards for the suite led with integration proof and honest ground where the suite won. Discovery questions added a line about how the buyer's team would absorb a change in workflow, which surfaced the implementation fear early. The vendor stopped discounting against the point tool on price, which it could not win, and started selling the migration path, which the buyers actually feared losing.

The market-structure question still sat above the deal work. Rivalry was fierce and the point tool kept entering on price. But once the vendor knew no-decision and implementation risk were the real competitors, it stopped fighting the wrong war.

## Common mistakes

**Analyzing the competitor leadership fears, not the ones you lose to.** The CEO names the market leader. The CRM shows you lose to no-decision and a niche tool. Analyze your real competitive set.

**Treating analysis as a one-time deliverable.** A comparison matrix shared at kickoff and never updated gives the organization false confidence. It is worse than no analysis because teams trust it. Run it as a continuous system. [1]

**Feature-matrix myopia.** Comparing checkboxes ignores positioning, pricing, go-to-market, and buyer perception. Two products with the same features can win very different deals. Analyze how they sell, not just what they ship.

**Ignoring no-decision and manual alternatives.** The buyer who does nothing and the team using spreadsheets are competitors. They often beat you more than any vendor. Name them and analyze them like any rival. [3]

**Pretending competitors have no strengths.** Battlecards that only list weaknesses destroy credibility. Reps who meet a genuine competitor strength on a live call and find it missing from the card stop trusting the whole program. [1]

**Trusting stated loss reasons.** "Lost on price" in the CRM is often a fear of failed implementation underneath. Triangulate rep report, buyer interview, and call data before you believe the reason. [4]

**No rep-facing output.** Analysis that stays in a research folder changes nothing. If findings do not reach battlecards, discovery questions, and objection frameworks, reps never see them and behavior never changes. [4]

**No ownership.** Without a named owner and a cadence, the program decays into a scramble after every lost deal. Assign ownership and set the monthly and quarterly rhythm. [1]
