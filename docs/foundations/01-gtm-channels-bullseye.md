# GTM Channels Bullseye

Find your single highest-use acquisition channel. Run a repeatable
five-step process that narrows every possible route to market down to
the one channel that unlocks your next stage of growth. Do not spread
effort across many channels. Pick one, go all-in, and scale it until it
saturates.

This page is an evolution of the classic Bullseye Framework from
*Traction* by Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, updated for the
2025-2026 market. The method is unchanged. The channel categories have
shifted as search, social, and AI changed where buyers are.

Every other channel page in this wiki assumes you have run this process
first. The [channels index](../channels/index.md) points to it as the
starting point for a reason. If you have not decided your one best
channel, you are not ready to read the deep dives.

---

## One Number That Matters Most

Most GTM failure is a distribution problem, not a product problem.
Peter Thiel put it plainly: it is very likely that one channel is
optimal, and most businesses actually get zero distribution channels to
work. Poor distribution, not product, is the number one cause of failure
([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)).

That is the core claim. The Bullseye exists to prevent the two most
common failure modes: trying every channel at once with no depth, and
committing to a single channel before you have tested it cheaply.

---

## Definition

The Bullseye Framework is a channel-selection method. It asks you to
consider every credible route to market, shortlist the most promising
options, run cheap tests on them, and then focus resources on what the
evidence says works.

It uses a three-ring diagram ([Rise Marketing, Bullseye Framework 2026](https://www.risemarketing.uk/post/the-bullseye-framework-what-is-it-and-how-to-use-it)):

- **Outer ring.** Channels that are possible but currently look less
  promising. You hold every channel here until you have a reason to
  move it inward.
- **Middle ring.** Channels that could work and deserve a test. These
  are your candidates.
- **Inner ring.** The small number of channels that look most promising
  and get tested first. The bullseye itself is the one channel you
  eventually focus on.

The purpose is not to pick a winner by discussion. It is to identify
the most sensible assumptions to test, then let the tests decide where
you concentrate time and budget.

The traction Bullseye is not the same as a brand-positioning bullseye.
A positioning bullseye defines what a brand stands for. The traction
Bullseye selects an acquisition channel. Positioning influences how
well your channel tests convert, but it does not tell you which channel
to run ([Rise Marketing, Bullseye Framework 2026](https://www.risemarketing.uk/post/the-bullseye-framework-what-is-it-and-how-to-use-it)).

## Why It Matters

The Bullseye exists because channel strategy is a decision, not a
default. Different phases of a company need different channels, and the
right channel changes as you grow.

Early on, small things move the needle. A single tweet from a respected
person or a talk in front of a few hundred people can produce a
meaningful jump in users. That same activity is invisible at scale. A
company with 10,000 daily visitors will not feel a blog post that sends
200 visitors. What moves the needle changes as you grow
([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)).

Two channel behaviors drive this ([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)):

- Some channels work early and fail later because they cannot produce
  the volume the business now needs.
- Some channels are hard to get working early but become major sources
  of traction later. Public relations is the classic example.

That is why the Bullseye is a repeatable process, not a one-time
decision. When your growth curve flattens, the channel that worked
before usually will not get you to the next level, and long-shot
channels from earlier may now be worth a retest.

The stakes are high. If you try several channels but nail none, you
finish. If you get even a single channel working, you have a real
business. The Bullseye is the system that gets you from zero to one
working channel as cheaply and quickly as possible.

## Framework: Five Steps

The Bullseye Framework is a five-step repeatable process: brainstorm,
rank, prioritize, test, and focus ([Traction, Bullseye Framework](https://traction.usefedora.com/courses/1415/lectures/26513)). Rinse and repeat.

### Step 1: Brainstorm (Diverge)

Generate at least one concrete idea for every major channel category.
Do not judge feasibility yet. The point of this step is to counteract
your channel biases.

If you would advertise offline, where would you place it? If you would
give a speech, who is the ideal audience? Everyone approaches the
channels with biases. This step forces you to hold a real idea for each
one rather than dismissing it out of hand.

Feed the brainstorm with research. Know what marketing strategies have
worked in your industry, how similar companies acquired customers over
time, and how unsuccessful companies wasted their marketing dollars.

For each idea, make an educated guess on three questions ([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)):

- What is the expected cost to acquire a customer through this channel?
- How many customers can you expect to acquire at that cost before
  saturation?
- What timeframe does a test require?

You will not know the exact answers. Make the guesses anyway. They force
comparison across channels.

### Step 2: Rank (Three Columns)

Place every channel into one of three columns ([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)):

- **Column A (Inner circle).** Channels that seem most promising right
  now.
- **Column B (Potential).** Channels that could plausibly work.
- **Column C (Long-shot).** Channels that seem like a stretch.

Let the research and ideas from step 1 guide the ranking. A few ideas
usually feel compelling. Those go in Column A. Channels with ideas that
could plausibly work go in Column B. The rest go in Column C.

### Step 3: Prioritize (Pick Three)

Identify the inner circle: the three channels that seem most promising.
If you already have three in Column A, you are done. If you have more,
cut some. If you have fewer, promote from Column B.

Draw the line where there is an obvious drop-off in excitement. That
drop-off usually occurs around the third channel
([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)).

Keep more than one channel in the inner circle. You do not want to waste
time testing channels sequentially when you can test in parallel. But
keep the number low, around three. Doing too many things in parallel
produces errors from lack of focus.

### Step 4: Test (Cheap, Fast, Measurable)

Put your three inner-circle channels into the real world with cheap,
fast tests. The goal is not to get a lot of traction yet. It is to
determine whether each channel could work for you.

Design each test to answer three questions ([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)):

- Roughly how much will it cost to acquire customers through this
  channel?
- How many customers are available through this channel?
- Are the customers you acquire through this channel ones you want?

Design tests that do not require much up-front cost or effort. You
should be able to get a rough read on a channel with a few hundred
dollars or a couple of weeks. Your main consideration is speed to data
and proof of assumption, not polished execution.

Document learnings even when a test fails. A failed test at low cost is
cheap information. A failed test you cannot learn from is waste.

### Step 5: Focus (Go All-In)

When the data points to one channel, commit. Pick the primary channel
and go all-in until it saturates. This is where the majority of your
customer acquisition is created.

When that channel stops producing at the volume you need, repeat the
process. The Bullseye is not a one-time exercise. It is the operating
loop for your entire go-to-market.

## How to Execute: 2026 Channel Categories

The original framework listed 19 traction channels. The method still
works, but the categories need a 2026 reinterpretation because the
channels have changed ([Rise Marketing, Bullseye Framework 2026](https://www.risemarketing.uk/post/the-bullseye-framework-what-is-it-and-how-to-use-it)):

- **Targeting blogs** is now borrowed audiences: specialists, industry
  newsletters, podcasts, YouTube channels, LinkedIn creators, Substack
  publications, and established communities.
- **Publicity** is now earned media and digital PR: expert commentary,
  original research, reactive PR, podcast interviews, data-led digital
  PR.
- **Unconventional PR** is now attention-led campaigns: stunts,
  unusual launches, provocative research findings, distinctive
  partnerships.
- **Search Engine Marketing** now includes paid search plus paid
  placements in emerging search and AI environments.
- **Social and display ads** now span Meta, LinkedIn, TikTok, YouTube,
  Reddit, programmatic, connected TV, and creator-amplified campaigns.
- **Email marketing** now sits inside broader lifecycle messaging that
  includes SMS, WhatsApp, push, and in-product messages.
- **Engineering as marketing** now includes calculators, audit tools,
  interactive assessments, and AI-powered utilities.
- **SEO** now includes visibility within AI-generated search and answer
  experiences.
- **Existing platforms** now include app marketplaces, review sites,
  creator platforms, comparison sites, and AI ecosystems.

### Channel test design checklist

A good Bullseye test is bounded and measurable. Use this checklist on
every inner-circle test ([Rise Marketing, Bullseye Framework 2026](https://www.risemarketing.uk/post/the-bullseye-framework-what-is-it-and-how-to-use-it)):

- Record the hypothesis before you spend.
- Name the audience you are testing against.
- State the proposition you are testing.
- Set the budget and timeframe up front. A couple of thousand dollars
  or two weeks is a reasonable box.
- Define the success metric before you launch.
- Document the result and the next decision, even on a failed test.

### Channel choice by company stage and segment

Your winning channel is not fixed. It changes by company stage and by
market segment. Plan for the channel to rotate as you grow
([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)).

- **Seed stage.** Pre-fit channels often look unscalable. Manual
  recruiting, founder relationships, and direct outreach produce the
  first users. Do the work that does not scale before you chase
  scalable channels.
- **Post-fit.** Once the product resonates, switch to the scalable
  channel the Bullseye tests surface. This is where most of your
  customer acquisition happens.
- **Scale stage.** The channel that got you here usually cannot carry
  the next phase. When growth flattens, rerun the Bullseye and expect
  the answer to be a different channel.
- **By segment.** Channel fit differs by buyer. High-ACV enterprise
  buyers respond to ABM and direct sales. Self-serve developers respond
  to community, integrations, and engineering marketing. Match the
  channel to how your specific buyer decides to purchase, not to a
  generic playbook.

The mistake is treating your first winning channel as permanent. Treat
it as the channel for this growth stage, then reassess.

### Situation-based starting channels

Your situation determines which channels are likely to rank high in
step 2. These are starting points, not defaults.

| Situation | Likely starting channels | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Early stage, low budget | Community, content, PLG | Lowest cost, highest learning |
| AI / developer tools | Engineering marketing, community, integrations | Developers trust peers and code |
| High-ACV B2B (over $50k) | ABM, targeted sales, intent data | Precision and relationships matter |
| Prosumer / creator tools | Short-form video, community, influencers | Authentic distribution wins |
| Marketplace / platform | Ecosystem, integrations, partnerships | Use existing user bases |

## Worked Example: Mint

The Bullseye in action is best seen through a real company. Mint set a
goal of 100,000 users in the first six months after launch.

In steps 1 through 3, Noah Kagan and his team brainstormed and picked
several promising channels: targeting blogs, public relations, and
search engine marketing.

In step 4, they ran cheap tests in each: sponsored a small newsletter,
reached out to financial celebrities, and placed some Google ads. The
tests showed which channels could work and which could not.

In step 5, Mint focused on the channel that looked most promising and
could reach their goal: targeting blogs. Sponsoring mid-level bloggers
in the financial niche and guest posting acquired their first 40,000
customers.

When that channel maxed out, they repeated the Bullseye and found a new
channel to focus on: public relations. Within six months of launch they
had one million users ([Brian Balfour, The Bullseye Framework](https://brianbalfour.com/essays/traction-the-bullseye-framework)).

That is the pattern. Research many channels, test a few, focus on the
most promising until it stops working, then repeat.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Testing too many channels at once.** More than three parallel
   tests produces errors from lack of focus. The whole point is to
   narrow, not to hedge.
2. **Scaling before understanding unit economics.** You do not know a
   channel works until you know its cost to acquire and whether the
   customers you get are the ones you want.
3. **Committing to a channel before testing it.** The most common
   mistake is deciding on a channel by opinion and then spending months
   scaling a bad bet. Cheap tests exist to prevent this.
4. **Treating the framework as a one-time decision.** Channels saturate
   and markets shift. When growth flattens, rerun the Bullseye.
5. **Treating community or any channel as a broadcast channel.** Some
   channels are relationship-based and do not respond to scale-as-broadcast.
6. **Copying what worked for a different business model.** A high-ACV
   enterprise seller and a self-serve PLG product do not share the same
   winning channel.
7. **Interpreting the framework to mean only one channel ever
   contributes.** The Bullseye picks your primary channel. Secondary
   channels still support the motion. The point is that one channel
   leads and gets the investment.

## Limitations

The Bullseye is a channel-selection model, not a full growth strategy.
It does not replace positioning, product-market fit, retention
planning, conversion optimization, or broader commercial strategy
([Rise Marketing, Bullseye Framework 2026](https://www.risemarketing.uk/post/the-bullseye-framework-what-is-it-and-how-to-use-it)).

Run it after you have a product people want. The framework assumes a
working product and a real audience. If you are still finding
product-market fit, your first channel is often the unscalable work of
recruiting users manually, as Paul Graham describes in *Do Things That
Don't Scale*.
