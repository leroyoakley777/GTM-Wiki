# Positioning

Positioning is the place your product occupies in a target customer's mind relative to alternatives. It answers four questions: who it is for, what problem it solves, what category they file it under, and why they would pick it over what they already do.

It is internal strategy. Messaging is the external expression. Copy is the sentence. Confusing those jobs is how GTM stalls: sales explains too much, ads convert nobody, and product ships features nobody asked for.

This page is the writable version. For the full Dunford walkthrough, see [Positioning and messaging](/docs/foundations/positioning-and-messaging).


## Why it comes before copy

Copy describes a position. It cannot invent one.

Stripe did not change the product when it moved from "payments infrastructure" to "economic infrastructure for the internet." The frame changed. The audience expanded from developers to CFOs and boards. The same mechanism works at small scale: a broker tool positioned as "institutional capital access for independent brokers" rather than "another marketplace" attracts producers who already have marketplaces and need capital.

Weak positioning shows up downstream as:

- Sales calls that start with a ten-minute category lesson.
- Pricing fights, because the buyer cannot name the differential value.
- Feature requests that chase whoever showed up, not the ICP.

Fix the position. Then write.


## Output you can write from

A position is ready for messaging when you can fill this card without hedging.

```text
Product:     [name]
Category:    [the market frame the buyer already uses, or the one you are creating]
Audience:    [best-fit customer, specific enough to find]
Alternative: [what they do today if you do not exist]
Value:       [the outcome only you deliver for that audience]
Proof:       [evidence a skeptic would accept]
Trend:       [optional: the change that makes this urgent now]
```

If a row is a slogan, you are not done. "Better user experience" is not a value. "Answer the customer while they are still on the phone" is a value.

Dunford's 2019/2024 method treats this card as the output of a process, not a fill-in-the-blank that assumes the answers are already in your head [197][257].


## Dunford process, compressed

April Dunford's five moves, in order [197][257]:

1. **Drop the origin story.** Most products start in one market and drift into another. Holding the original category highlights what you cannot do and puts you against entrenched incumbents.
2. **List uniqueness without judging it.** Features, business model, process, IP, even complexity. Capture the raw set first.
3. **Translate uniqueness into value.** Customers buy outcomes. "Patented fast query on massive datasets" becomes "answer customer questions while they are still on the phone."
4. **Name who cares a lot.** Many people like the value. A few urgently need it. Industry, size, stack, buying motion, pain intensity. "SMBs" is not an audience.
5. **Pick the market frame.** The category tells buyers how to evaluate you. An existing category ramps faster. A new category raises the ceiling and the cost.

The competitive alternative is often not a vendor. It is an intern, a spreadsheet, or doing nothing [257]. That alternative sets the minimum bar you have to beat.


## Moore statement, as an alignment tool

Geoffrey Moore's 2014 template is for internal alignment, not a homepage hero [225]. It forces the same choices onto one page:

```text
For [target customer]
who [need or opportunity],
the [product name] is a [category]
that [key benefit].
Unlike [primary alternative],
our product [primary differentiation].
```

Rules that keep it from rotting:

- Target customer is a role with context, not "teams."
- Need is a problem they already feel, not a feature list.
- Category is a bucket they already have, unless you are deliberately creating one.
- Benefit is an outcome.
- Alternative is what they use today.
- Differentiation is defensible, not "easier to use."

Do not put this paragraph on a website. Put it in the doc the writers and sellers share. Messaging turns it into language a buyer can hear.


## How positioning changes the words

Once the card is filled, copy has constraints:

- **One category per asset.** Mixing "we are a platform and a workflow and an AI copilot" teaches the reader nothing.
- **Lead with the value, not the feature.** Features belong in a later proof block or a comparison table.
- **Name the alternative.** Buyers compare you to something. If you do not name it, they will pick a comparison that hurts you.
- **Proof has to match the claim.** A bigger claim needs a harder proof. Superlatives are not proof.

Copy that ignores the card reads as generic even when the sentences are fluent.


## Failure modes

- **Positioning as a tagline.** A punchy line with no category, alternative, or proof. Fix: fill the card. Then write the line.
- **Positioning as a feature list.** Ten differentiators, no audience. Fix: pick the one value the best-fit customer will pay for.
- **Copy as a substitute.** Rewriting the homepage weekly because win rates are low. Fix: the [ICP](/docs/foundations/icp-definition) and this card first.
- **Category soup.** A new name nobody can file. Fix: borrow a known category unless you have the budget to teach a new one [257].
- **Competing with a ghost.** Positioning against a vendor the buyer does not consider. Fix: start from what they would do if you disappeared.

## Variants by company stage

- **Seed.** Founder can say the card out loud in one breath. If they cannot, outbound and ads will invent a pitch on every call. Do not hire writers to cover for this.
- **Early team.** The card is written, shared with sales and marketing, and used to reject off-ICP deals. Messaging can now specialize by persona without changing the position.
- **Mid-market.** Multiple products or segments. Each segment gets its own card. Do not run one generic position across two ICPs.
- **Enterprise.** Category and alternative often shift to "rip and replace" versus "do nothing." Proof has to survive a security review and a CFO. The card still has one value.

## Agentic layer

An agent can draft candidate cards from interviews, win/loss notes, and competitor pages. It cannot decide the category or the alternative. Those are bets.

```text
SOP: draft a positioning card from evidence

Given: ICP line, top three win reasons, top three loss reasons,
named alternatives from recent deals, one proof point that is true.
1. Fill every row of the positioning card.
2. Flag any row that is a slogan rather than a decision.
3. List the alternative the buyer would use if the product vanished.
4. Return the card and the two weakest rows.
Never invent a proof point.
Never invent a category the company has not chosen.
```

A human picks the category and owns the bet. The agent prepares the options.
