# Messaging

Messaging translates positioning into language a buyer hears. Positioning decides the place you occupy. Messaging decides the claim, the frame, and the proof that travel through website, sales call, ad, and deck.

It is still not copy. Copy is the sentence. Messaging is the approved meaning those sentences have to carry. Dunford (2019, restated 2024) treats positioning as context, not a tagline; messaging is what the buyer hears after that context is set [257].


## Job of a message

A message has to do four things at once:

1. Name the reader.
2. Name the problem they already feel.
3. Name the outcome you cause.
4. Give one reason to believe it.

If you cannot write those four lines, you are not ready to pick a framework. A framework applied to a muddy claim produces muddy copy that is easier to read and no more convincing.

Product Marketing Alliance's working rules match that bar: lead with the problem, be specific, use the buyer's language, let proof beat adjectives, and keep one idea per asset [224].


## Claim template

Fill this before anyone writes a headline.

```text
For [ICP] who [felt problem],
we [category / motion]
so they [outcome].
Proof: [named result, number, or customer].
Objection we will hear: [the doubt].
Reply: [the true answer, not a dodge].
```

Moore's structure sits underneath: for a target who has a need, this product is a category that delivers a benefit [225]. Messaging is the buyer-facing compression of that internal statement.

Rules:

- **One claim per asset.** A hero, a cold first touch, a slide, an ad. Stacking claims creates none [224].
- **The problem is theirs, not yours.** "We built a platform that..." is a product tour. "Review time blows past the SLA after you hire the third AppSec engineer" is a problem they recognize.
- **Proof is specific.** "Customers love us" is an adjective. A named customer plus a named time or dollar change is a proof point.
- **The objection is written down.** If you cannot name the doubt, the buyer will, and you will improvise.


## Message pillars

Most B2B products need 3 to 5 pillars, not 15 taglines. Each pillar maps to one differentiator from the [positioning card](/docs/copywriting/positioning).

| Pillar field | What it holds |
|--------------|---------------|
| Headline | The claim in the buyer's words |
| Felt problem | The cost of the status quo |
| Outcome | What changes if the claim is true |
| Proof | A number, a named customer, a demoable result |
| Objection | What a skeptic says next |
| Used when | Which persona or stage this pillar leads |

Keep the same pillars across personas. Shift emphasis. The economic buyer hears risk and payback. The user hears time back. The technical evaluator hears how it works and what it breaks. Amplitude's buying-committee research puts typical B2B committees at 7-8 people with different evaluation criteria. One pillar set, several cuts.

Do not invent a new position per persona. That is how the website says one thing and the AE says another.


## Frame of reference

Every message sits in a comparison the buyer already has. You pick it or they will.

- **Direct competitor.** Use when they are already shopping the category. Messaging has to say why you, not why the category.
- **Status quo / do nothing.** Use when they have not decided to buy anything. Messaging has to make the cost of waiting concrete.
- **New category.** Use only when you will spend to teach the frame. Otherwise they will file you under the nearest familiar bucket, usually a cheaper one.

The frame decides the first line of every asset. A competitor frame leads with difference. A status-quo frame leads with cost of the current path. A new-category frame leads with the problem the old categories fail.


## Voice constraints that survive every surface

These are messaging rules, not style tips:

- Use the words the buyer uses in interviews and win/loss notes, not the words the product team prefers [224].
- Cut unearned superlatives. If a claim needs "leading" or "best" to work, the proof is missing.
- Prefer nouns and verbs over atmosphere. "Cuts reconciliation from two days to 20 minutes" beats "transforms finance operations."
- One ask per asset, matching the stage. A first touch asks for a next step. A pricing page asks for a plan. Mixing asks gives the reader a way to stall.

These rules apply to ads, pages, decks, and outbound alike. They are not a license to paste a recommended email. Outbound still has to earn a reason-to-reach per row. See [Outbound](/docs/channels/outbound).


## Failure modes

- **Messaging as a synonym for copy.** A Google Doc of headlines with no positioning card underneath. Fix: fill [positioning](/docs/copywriting/positioning) first.
- **One claim, many messages.** Three value props in one hero. Fix: one claim on the asset; the rest wait for a later page or a later conversation [224].
- **Proof-free ambition.** Big outcome, no named result. Fix: shrink the claim to the proof you have, or get the proof.
- **Internal jargon on a buyer page.** "Unified orchestration layer" to a VP who said "our reps work out of four tools." Fix: borrow their sentence.
- **Persona drift.** Marketing writes for the user. Sales sells to the economic buyer. Fix: same pillars, different emphasis, same card.

## Variants by company stage

- **Seed.** One claim. One pillar. Founder language is the messaging doc. Do not run a committee process on a 12-person company.
- **Early team.** Two or three pillars, written down, used in onboarding. Sales and marketing share the claim template.
- **Mid-market.** Pillars plus persona cuts. Product marketing owns the doc. Outbound and ads pull from it; they do not freelance a new offer.
- **Enterprise.** Committee-aware cuts of the same claim. Technical proof sits in FAB-style assets. Economic-buyer proof sits in payback and risk. See [Frameworks](/docs/copywriting/frameworks).

## Agentic layer

An agent can assemble a claim template from the positioning card and from interview quotes. It cannot pick the claim.

```text
SOP: draft a messaging card

Given: a filled positioning card, 5 buyer quotes, one true proof point,
and the surface (homepage hero, ad, deck opener). Not an email.
1. Fill the claim template in the buyer's words.
2. Attach one proof point. If none was given, stop.
3. Write the likely objection and the true reply.
4. Return the card. Do not return a finished email, ad, or landing page.
Never invent a customer result.
Never add a second claim.
```

Humans approve the claim. Writers (human or agent) fill a framework only after that approval.
