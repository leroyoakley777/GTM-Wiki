# GTM Metrics Benchmarks

**The empirical check on your metric stack.** This page pairs with the [GTM Metrics](../foundations/05-gtm-metrics.md) foundation — that page defines what the metrics are and how to steer with them; this page gives you the *numbers* to sanity-check your model against. Median B2B SaaS is not efficient, and the benchmarks below tell you where average sits.

These are **baselines, not targets**. Every number is a range or a median with a named source. Where a single number appears, it is reported — never treated as law.

---

## The Numbers That Matter Most

**Win rate is the highest-leverage number on the board.** A 1–3 point shift in win rate changes required pipeline coverage and generation more than any other single input (Benchmarkit / GTM founders). Average across B2B is ~21% for all opportunities and ~29% for qualified ones — which is exactly why coverage must be read against *your* conversion math, not a fixed multiple.

**Pipeline coverage is the top board metric — and it is directional, not law.** The "3x" rule is a rule of thumb, not physics. RevOps teams hit quota at 1.8x coverage and miss at 4x, depending on win rate, cycle length, and push rate (Janis Zech). Read coverage against your own funnel, never in isolation.

## The Metrics That Matter, and Why

### Pipeline coverage — the top board metric

3–4x mid-market, 4–5x enterprise. But coverage is a *function* of win rate, cycle length, and push rate — not an input. A team that wins 40% of qualified opportunities needs far less coverage than one that wins 15%. The 3x benchmark is where average companies land, not where you should aim blindly. Read it against your own conversion math.

### Pipe-to-spend — the budget governor

Below 3x means the marketing engine is structurally broken — spend is not producing enough qualified pipeline. Above 10x usually means you are under-investing. The ratio tells you whether marketing is *allowed* to spend more, not whether it is efficient.

### NRR — the retention gate

Enterprise median NRR (118%) crushes SMB (97%) — expansion is where enterprise value lives. Companies with ≥100% NRR grew ~48% YoY on average (ChartMogul H1 2024). NRR below 100% means your existing base is shrinking and you must out-acquire the leak just to stay flat.

### Quota attainment — the calibration check

~43–47% average across B2B (RepVue Q4 2024: 43.14%; Forrester ~47%). This is the single best signal of whether quotas are set correctly:

- Reps consistently at **70–85%** → quotas are well-calibrated.
- Above **85%** → quotas are too low (you are leaving money on the table).
- Below **70%** → quotas are too high or the model is broken.

The ~43–47% *average* is not a target — it reflects a market where most companies set quotas slightly too high.

## Cross-Cutting Findings

These hold across multiple sources and reshape how you read the table above.

### The efficiency era reset pre-2023 targets

CAC payback expectations tightened from 18–24 months to **12 months**. Post-ZIRP boards judge pipe-to-spend and payback before growth. A 2020 benchmark is a liability in 2026 — always check the vintage of the number you are quoting.

### Stage changes the economics

Payback drops monotonically with ARR:

- Sub-$1M ARR → **21-month** payback
- $1–10M → **16-month**
- $10–50M → **13-month**
- $50M+ → **11-month**

LTV:CAC climbs the same ladder (2.4x → 4.2x). **Benchmarks without an ARR cohort are noise.** A sub-$1M SaaS at 2.4x LTV:CAC is normal; the same number at $50M+ ARR is a fire.

### Sales-led vs self-serve CAC is a 16x spread

$11,400 vs $702. This is a **motion choice, not an efficiency gap.** Never compare a sales-led CAC to a self-serve benchmark — you will panic about a number that is structurally different, not worse.

### Win rate is the highest-leverage number

A 1–3 point shift changes required pipeline coverage and generation more than any other input (Benchmarkit / GTM founders). If you are going to tune one number, tune this one first.

---

## How to Read These Numbers

1. **Baselines, not targets.** "~21% win rate" is what average companies do, not a number to engineer toward. Use these to sanity-check your own model, then set targets against your motion and stage.
2. **Segment before panicking.** A sub-$1M SaaS at 2.4x LTV:CAC and 21-month payback is normal; the same numbers at $50M+ ARR are a fire.
3. **Pair every metric.** CAC alone is meaningless — pair with payback and LTV:CAC. Coverage alone is meaningless — pair with win rate and cycle.
4. **Gross-margin LTV against fully-loaded CAC** is the only version of LTV:CAC that means anything.

---

## The Pattern Behind the Numbers

The 2026 efficiency era has a clear signature: **tight payback, high coverage, ruthless NRR.** Boards stopped rewarding growth at any cost and started rewarding growth that pays for itself. The benchmarks that survive — 12-month payback, 5–8x pipe-to-spend, 3.2:1 LTV:CAC, ≥100% NRR — all point the same direction: acquire efficiently, retain fiercely, expand relentlessly.

If your numbers sit inside these ranges, you are average — and average is beatable. If they sit outside on the wrong side, the leak is named. The point of a benchmark is not to feel good or bad about your number. It is to tell you *where* to work.
