# Messaging and Copywriting

Messaging and copywriting is the skill of turning who you sell to into words that work. You run it before any email, landing page, ad, or pitch goes out. The words are the last layer, not the first. Three layers sit under them, and each one decides whether the words land.

1. **Audience.** Who you are selling to and why. The person and the reason they should care.
2. **Messaging and positioning.** What you are communicating. The value claim, the frame, the proof.
3. **Copywriting.** The words on the page. Structured by a framework that moves the reader from a state to an action.

Most bad copy fails at layer 1 or 2 and blames layer 3. You cannot write your way out of a wrong audience or an unclear claim. Fix the layers underneath first, then write.

This page teaches the three-layer model and then goes deep on the frameworks that structure copy, with the email frameworks as the centerpiece. The artifact is the framework map and the selection grid, not a finished email. You pick the framework from the reader's decision problem, then fill it with your audience and your claim.

## When to use this skill

Run messaging and copywriting when you are about to write something a buyer reads:

- A cold email or a follow-up sequence.
- A landing page, homepage, or pricing page.
- An ad, a social post, or a launch email.
- A sales deck or a demo script.
- Any piece where the reader has to decide to act.

Do not start with the words. Start with who you are writing to and what you are claiming. A framework applied to a muddy claim produces muddy copy that is easier to read and no more convincing.

## Definition

Messaging and copywriting is the repeatable process of converting audience insight and a value claim into reader-facing words, structured by a persuasion framework that matches the reader's decision problem. The meeting, the click, or the reply is the unit of output, not the sentence.

## Why it matters

Copy is where GTM decisions become reader-facing reality. A defined ICP drives 68% higher win rates and 30% more marketing-sourced revenue than an undefined one [1], but the claim still has to reach the buyer in words they act on. Positioning that is clear internally produces nothing until it is written for a specific reader.

The stakes are measurable. A signal-based, personalized cold email reaches 15-25% reply rates versus low single digits for a generic blast [2]; 50-125 word emails earn about 50% higher reply rates than long templated sends [3]. The same offer, different words, different result. The frameworks exist because the difference is not talent. It is structure.

## Three-layer model

Work the layers in order. Each one constrains the next.

### Layer 1: Audience, who you are selling to and why

Before words, name the reader and the reason they should care.

- **The person.** The title, the role, and the job they are trying to do. Write to the economic buyer, the user, and the technical evaluator separately; they decide on different grounds [4].
- **The why.** The problem they feel now and the cost of not solving it. The reader is problem-aware before they are solution-aware. Lead with the pain they recognize, not your product [5].
- **The proof they will trust.** A customer result, a data point, or a recognized use case carries more weight than a superlative [5].

The output of this layer is one line you can say about the reader: who they are, what they are trying to fix, and why it costs them to ignore it. If you cannot say that line, you are not ready to write.

### Layer 2: Messaging and positioning, what you are communicating

This layer is the value claim and the frame it sits in. It comes from your positioning work, not from your keyboard.

- **The value claim.** For [ICP] who [pain], we provide [solution] that [outcome] [6].
- **The frame of reference.** Who the buyer compares you to: direct competitors, the status quo, or a new category [6].
- **The proof points.** The 2-3 facts that back the claim: a result, a number, a named customer [5].
- **The objection.** What the reader will doubt and the honest answer.

Keep this to one claim per asset. A cold email makes one claim. A hero section makes one claim. A slide makes one claim. Stacking messages creates none [5].

### Layer 3: Copywriting, the words

This is where you write. Pick a framework that matches the reader's decision problem, fill it with layer 1 and layer 2, and write the words the framework calls for. The rest of this page is the deep dive on those frameworks.

## Deep dive: the copywriting frameworks

A framework is a move order. It decides what the reader feels and in what sequence, and it ends with a request for a specific action. Frameworks differ in length, in the depth of the emotional build, and in which reader decision problem they solve. Match the framework to the problem, not to your favorite formula.

### Decision-based selection grid

| Reader's decision problem | Framework | Structure |
|---|---|---|
| Aware of the problem, needs a quick fix | PAS | Problem, Agitate, Solution |
| Cold, needs to feel the stakes, low trust | AIDA | Attention, Interest, Desire, Action |
| Transformation offer with a visible before and after | BAB | Before, After, Bridge |
| Comparing options, needs product logic | FAB | Feature, Advantage, Benefit |
| Short-form ad, needs a fast sequence to action | 4Ps | Promise, Picture, Proof, Push |
| High-ticket, needs trust and a story before the ask | PASTOR | Problem, Amplify, Story, Transformation, Offer, Response |
| Needs urgency in a short form | 4 Us | Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific |

A common funnel sequence is 4Ps for the hook, BAB for the opening transformation, PASTOR for the main sales narrative, and FAB for offer details or objections [7].

### PAS: Problem, Agitate, Solution

**What it is.** A reliable framework for an audience that already feels the problem. Name the problem, intensify its cost, then present the fix. Short, hard to get wrong, covers most everyday copy [8].

**When to use it.** Cold emails, short ads, problem-aware audiences, any copy under roughly 300 words. It is the beginner default because it is short and the move order is unforgivingly clear [8].

**The move order.**
- **Problem.** Name the specific frustration the reader feels now. Be precise, not generic. A problem that captures attention does the work an attention hook would do [8].
- **Agitate.** Make the cost of the problem staying unsolved concrete. This is where the reader decides they have to act. Agitate with the real cost, not exaggeration; hype costs trust [9].
- **Solution.** Present the offer as the direct fix, with one proof point or concrete result.

**Where it fails.** The writer skips the problem and leads with the solution, or agitates into melodrama. If the reader does not feel the problem, PAS has nothing to build on. Start with layer 1: verify the reader actually feels the pain before you agitate it.

### AIDA: Attention, Interest, Desire, Action

**What it is.** The oldest and most general framework, credited to advertising pioneer E. St. Elmo Lewis. Attention is the hook, interest holds it, desire makes the reader want the outcome, action asks for the step [10].

**When to use it.** Longer pieces, sales pages, launch emails, and any reader who is not yet committed to the problem. AIDA gives you room to build interest and desire before the ask [10].

**The move order.**
- **Attention.** Stop the reader with a claim, a question, or a concrete observation tied to their situation.
- **Interest.** Hold them with the reason this matters to them, not to you.
- **Desire.** Make them want the outcome. This is where proof, specificity, and the picture of the result live.
- **Action.** Ask for the specific next step in one line.

**Where it fails.** Attention that is a stunt with no follow-through, or desire that stays abstract. The reader stops, then drifts. Interest and desire need proof and specificity, which come from layer 2.

### BAB: Before, After, Bridge

**What it is.** Paint the current state, paint the better state, then show the path between them. The contrast is the mechanism [7].

**When to use it.** Transformation offers, coaching, courses, results-oriented software, and any offer with a visible before and after. Best when the reader can picture their life after the change [7].

**The move order.**
- **Before.** The state the reader is in now, described specifically enough that they recognize it.
- **After.** The state they move to, concrete and sensory.
- **Bridge.** Your offer as the path between the two, with the mechanism and a reason it is credible.

**Where it fails.** The After is vague or the Bridge is a leap. The reader recognizes the Before, wants the After, and does not believe the path. Close the gap with proof from layer 2.

### FAB: Feature, Advantage, Benefit

**What it is.** The feature is what the product has. The advantage is why that matters. The benefit is what it changes for the buyer. FAB forces the translation from what you built to what they get [7].

**When to use it.** Comparison content, pricing tables, implementation notes, objection handling, and feature-heavy products where the instinct is to list everything the product does [7].

**The move order.**
- **Feature.** What the product has.
- **Advantage.** Why that feature matters in use.
- **Benefit.** What it changes for the buyer's outcome.

**Where it fails.** The writer stops at Feature, listing capability instead of value. Every feature row must end in a benefit in the buyer's terms, or the reader does the translation themselves and usually stops.

### 4Ps: Promise, Picture, Proof, Push

**What it is.** A short-form sequence that moves fast: the claim, the imagined result, the credibility, and the action [7].

**When to use it.** Ads, hero sections, short blocks, and any copy under roughly 90 words where momentum matters more than diagnosis [7].

**The move order.**
- **Promise.** One bold, specific, believable claim tied to the reader's main desire.
- **Picture.** A short scene of the result after the promise holds.
- **Proof.** A real proof element: a number, a testimonial, a result. The more ambitious the promise, the more specific the proof must be [7].
- **Push.** A direct call to action with a reason to act now.

**Where it fails.** A promise with thin proof. The Picture raises expectations the Proof does not meet, and the reader disengages. Match proof weight to promise size.

### PASTOR: Problem, Amplify, Story, Transformation, Offer, Response

**What it is.** The long-form framework created by copywriter Ray Edwards. It earns trust before the ask by adding a story and a named transformation [11].

**When to use it.** High-ticket offers, sales pages, long emails, and anything trust-sensitive where the sale needs objection handling and a structured close. If PAS is the jab, PASTOR is the full combination [8].

**The move order.**
- **Problem.** Name the problem the reader is stuck in.
- **Amplify.** Show the consequence of leaving it unsolved.
- **Story and Solution.** A story of someone who faced the problem and the solution that helped them. The story is the step most writers skip, and it is the step that earns trust [8].
- **Transformation.** The shift the reader can expect, named in outcomes.
- **Offer.** What you are offering, what it costs, and what it includes.
- **Response.** The specific next step.

**Where it fails.** The writer skips the Story and runs a six-step PAS. Without the story, the length feels like padding instead of trust-building. The story is the mechanism, not decoration.

### 4 Us: Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific

**What it is.** A filter for a single hook or subject line, not a full structure. Check your hook against four tests: is it useful, urgent, unique, and ultra-specific [7].

**When to use it.** Subject lines, headlines, and the first line of any asset where you have one chance to earn the read.

**The move order.** Score your proposed hook on each U. Rewrite the one that scores lowest until all four pass. A subject line that is useful and specific but not urgent gets rewritten for urgency.

**Where it fails.** Treating it as a structure instead of a filter. The 4 Us do not build a case; they sharpen a single sentence.

## Choosing the framework for email

Email is where the frameworks earn their keep, and the selection follows the reader's decision problem, not the stage of your sequence. This is the map for the cold email and sequence context from the [outbound skill](./outbound).

- **First touch, cold, problem-aware.** PAS. The reader feels the pain, so you name it, agitate it, and present the fix in under 125 words [3].
- **First touch, cold, not yet committed.** AIDA or 4Ps. You have to build interest and desire before you ask, or create momentum toward the action in a short form [7].
- **Transformation offer.** BAB. The before and after is the whole case.
- **Follow-up, when the reader is comparing.** FAB. They are weighing options and need product logic.
- **Long, high-ticket, trust-sensitive.** PASTOR. You need the story and the objection handling before the ask.

A sequence typically mixes frameworks across touches because the reader's decision problem changes as they move from cold to evaluating. The first touch solves "do I read this," the middle touches solve "is this real," and the last touch solves "do I act now."

## Failure modes and how to fix them

- **Writing before the layers.** Copy that is fluent and unconvincing. Fix: name the reader and the claim in one line each before you write. If you cannot, the framework has nothing to fill.
- **Framework as decoration.** Applying PAS on top of a muddy claim. Fix: layer 1 and 2 first. The framework exposes a weak claim; it does not fix it.
- **One framework for everything.** Running PAS on a high-ticket offer that needs trust, or PASTOR on a 60-word ad. Fix: pick from the decision grid, not your favorite formula [7].
- **Agitation into hype.** Overstating the pain until the reader disbelieves the copy. Fix: agitate with the concrete cost and let the proof carry it [9].
- **The skipped story in PASTOR.** Running a six-step PAS and calling it PASTOR. Fix: the story is the mechanism. Without it the length reads as padding [8].
- **One claim, many messages.** Stacking three value claims in one asset. Fix: one claim per asset; the rest goes on a later page or a later touch [5].

## How the skill differs by segment and maturity

- **By segment.** SMB buyers decide faster and respond to shorter, benefit-led copy (BAB, 4Ps). Enterprise involves a committee and an evaluator who needs product logic, so FAB for the technical proof and PASTOR for the economic buyer's trust [4].
- **By maturity.** A new motion should test one framework across a small set and read the reply and conversion data before scaling. A mature motion can run a framework map across segments and let the agent fill templates.
- **By channel pairing.** Messaging feeds every channel. Pair it with [outbound](./outbound) for the cold sequence, [prospecting](./prospecting) for the audience layer, and the [positioning and messaging foundation](../foundations/03-positioning-and-messaging.md) for layer 2.

## Agentic layer

An agent can draft copy from a framework, but it cannot decide who you are selling to or what you are claiming. Give it layer 1 and layer 2; it will write layer 3. Keep the audience call, the value claim, and the final human pass with you.

**SOP: write copy from the framework map.**

```text
Given: a named reader (audience line), a value claim (messaging line), a proof point,
and a target asset (cold email, ad, landing page, pitch).
1. Choose the framework from the reader's decision problem (decision grid).
2. Fill the framework's move order with the audience line, the claim, and the proof.
3. Keep it to one claim. One ask at the end.
4. Score the hook against the 4 Us; rewrite the weakest U.
5. Check against the taste gate: no superlatives, no hype, active voice.
6. Return the draft and the framework used, so a human can verify the layer-2 claim.
Never invent a proof point. Never assert a claim you were not given.
```

**Prompt: draft a cold email on PAS.**

```text
You are a direct-response copywriter. Write a cold email for a named reader and a
given value claim.

Use PAS:
- Problem: the specific frustration the reader feels now, precise, not generic.
- Agitate: the concrete cost of the problem staying unsolved, honest, not exaggerated.
- Solution: the offer as the direct fix, with one proof point.

Under 125 words. Lead with the problem, not the product. No superlatives, no
"I hope this finds you well." Output only the email.
```

Keep the human in the loop for the audience definition, the value claim, any compliance-sensitive send, and the final read before send. The agent drafts and checks; you decide and own the relationship.

## Run it today

Write the two lines now: the audience line (who the reader is and why they should care) and the messaging line (the claim and one proof point). Pick the framework from the decision grid that matches the reader's decision problem. Fill the framework. Score the hook against the 4 Us. Send nothing until the two lines are true and the proof is real.
