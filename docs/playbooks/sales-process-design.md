# Sales Process Design

Design a sales process that produces predictable revenue: seven stages, each with a hard exit gate, mapped to how buyers actually make decisions.

This playbook runs when a company has no sales process or when the existing one produces unreliable forecasts. Execute the steps in order, with an acceptance gate at each step. Skip a gate and the next step gets more expensive, not faster.

A sales process is the repeatable sequence of stages your revenue team follows to turn prospects into customers [1]. A sales process defines what steps your team takes. A sales methodology defines how they execute within each step [2]. You might run a seven-step process while using SPIN Selling for discovery and MEDDIC for qualification rigor [2].

## Step 0: Decide what the process has to carry

A sales process has one job: turn selling from reactive guesswork into a system that produces predictable results [1]. It delivers three outcomes: predictability, repeatability, and scalability [1].

Before you design anything, answer three questions:

- What is your **average deal size**? SMB deals close in 30 to 45 days, mid-market in 60 to 90 days, and enterprise in 120 to 180+ days [1]. The process complexity must match the deal complexity.
- How many **stakeholders** touch a deal? B2B purchase decisions typically involve 5 to 11 stakeholders [1], and the average B2B deal now involves 22 people including 13 internal stakeholders and 9 external participants [2].
- What **qualification framework** will you use? BANT (Budget, Authority, Need, Timeline) works for simple deals. MEDDIC works for complex, multi-stakeholder deals [1].

If you cannot answer these, stop and find the answers first. Designing a process against fuzzy inputs pays to speed up a mistake.

## Seven stages

Every company customizes stages to fit their market, but these seven represent a framework used by high-performing B2B revenue teams [1]. Most B2B teams perform best with 5 to 7 pipeline stages; fewer than five and you lose visibility, more than seven and reps resist updating stages [2].

| # | Stage | Goal | Exit criteria |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prospect | Identify high-fit accounts | Qualified list with confirmed contacts and research |
| 2 | Qualify | Validate fit and map stakeholders | Lead meets criteria, stakeholder map documented |
| 3 | Discover | Understand buyer needs | Documented needs confirmed by buyer |
| 4 | Present | Prove value | Buyer confirms fit and requests proposal |
| 5 | Propose | Formalize terms | Buyer confirms review date and approval process |
| 6 | Negotiate | Finalize agreement | All stakeholders approved, close date confirmed |
| 7 | Close | Secure signature and hand off | Deal closed, customer introduced to success team |

[1]

A deal advances when it meets the exit criteria for its stage. Exit criteria prevent reps from guessing, keep pipelines accurate, and make forecasts reliable [1].

Buyers now do 60 to 70% of their research before ever talking to a sales rep [3]. Your process must account for this reality. A common mistake is pushing demos before discovery, showing solutions before understanding problems [3].

### Stage 1: Prospect

Identify and prioritize accounts that match your ideal customer profile. The goal is to generate a list of accounts most likely to buy and succeed with your solution, not the longest list possible [1].

Build prioritized target lists with confirmed contact information. Use intent data and signals to prioritize accounts showing active buying signals [1].

**Exit criteria:** A qualified list of accounts with confirmed contact information and initial research on pain points, stakeholders, and potential fit [1].

### Stage 2: Qualify

Determine whether a prospect is worth investing more time in. Apply BANT or MEDDIC criteria. Map the buying committee to understand who influences the decision, who approves the budget, who will use the product, and who might block the deal [1].

Reps disqualify early and without guilt. A no now saves a stalled deal consuming pipeline space for three months [2].

**Exit criteria:** The lead meets your qualification criteria, and you have a map of decision-makers, influencers, and champions with their individual priorities documented [1].

One company tightened the exit criteria for their Qualified stage, requiring documented budget confirmation before deals could advance. This change removed low-intent deals from the pipeline earlier, improving their win rate by 8% [3].

### Stage 3: Discover

Understand the buyer's pain points, goals, and success criteria. Discovery is about listening, not selling. Ask open-ended questions and probe deeper to uncover the real issues behind surface-level answers [1].

Document pain points and priorities as they emerge. Align on success metrics that both sides can measure. Identify objections early so there are no surprises later [1].

**Exit criteria:** A documented understanding of the buyer's needs, priorities, and decision criteria, with confirmation from the buyer that you have captured it accurately [1].

One IT security vendor re-sequenced their process to put discovery before demo, which allowed them to tailor presentations to specific risk profiles. Win rates improved by 12% [3].

### Stage 4: Present

Show how your solution solves the buyer's specific problems. This is a tailored presentation that maps directly to what you learned in discovery, not a generic product demo [1].

Highlight relevant examples from similar customers. Quantify the business case. Address initial objections head-on [1].

**Exit criteria:** The buyer confirms the solution fits their needs and agrees to move forward, typically by requesting a proposal or pricing [1].

### Stage 5: Propose

Formalize what you have discussed, including pricing, scope, timeline, and terms. Confirm mutual commitment to move forward and map out the path to close [1].

**Exit criteria:** The buyer has received the proposal, confirmed they will review it by a specific date, and outlined their internal approval process with named stakeholders [1].

### Stage 6: Negotiate

Finalize pricing, terms, and contract details while navigating internal approvals on both sides. Address objections and concerns, adjust terms strategically, and coordinate internal approvals [1].

Track approval status and set a confirmed target close date [1].

**Exit criteria:** All stakeholders on both sides have approved, the contract is ready to sign, and the close date is confirmed [1].

### Stage 7: Close

Get the contract signed and hand off full context to the post-sales team. Closing is more than getting a signature; it ensures a smooth handoff to customer success so the customer achieves the value they were promised [1].

**Exit criteria:** Deal closed, customer introduced to success team [1].

## Design principles

### Stages mirror the buyer, not the seller

Your sales stages should reflect how buyers actually make decisions, not how sellers prefer to work [1]. The modern B2B buyer's journey moves through problem awareness, solution exploration, requirements building, vendor selection, validation, and onboarding [3].

Map buyer questions to sales actions at each journey phase. When the buyer asks "What exactly is my problem?" you share relevant content and ask discovery questions. When they ask "What approaches exist?" you provide education, not pitches [3].

### Exit criteria are the load-bearing wall

Without exit criteria, reps interpret stages differently, forecasts become unreliable, and deals stall without anyone noticing [2]. One SaaS company found that 40% of their Qualified deals had never confirmed budget; their stage definition was too loose [3].

### Handoffs are where deals die

Deals slip through the cracks when handoffs are informal [1]. Document customer goals before close. Automate notifications to legal and finance. Ensure customer success has full visibility from day 1 [1].

One professional services firm found that 25% of qualified leads never received timely follow-up because of unclear ownership [3].

### CRM is where the process lives

Your sales process only works if it lives in your CRM [2]. Build the process inside the CRM rather than documenting it separately. Define exit criteria for each stage. Use automation to reduce manual work. Create dashboards for visibility. Review the process quarterly based on data and rep feedback [1].

## Common failure modes

**Vague stage definitions.** When Qualified means different things to different reps, pipeline data becomes meaningless [3].

**Inconsistent qualification.** Without standardized criteria, reps spend too much time on deals that were never going to close. One manufacturing company discovered that reps were spending 35% of their time on prospects outside their ICP [3].

**Poor CRM hygiene.** Data entry gets skipped. Next steps go unlogged. Pipeline reviews become guesswork instead of analysis [3].

**Siloed teams.** When marketing, sales, and customer success operate independently, leads fall through cracks and customer relationships suffer at handoff points [3].

**Single-threaded deals.** If your champion leaves the company during the close, a single-threaded deal dies immediately. Build relationships with at least 3 to 4 contacts within the buying committee [2].

## Worked example: designing a process for a mid-market SaaS company

You sell a workflow automation tool at $25k ACV. Your deals involve 6 to 8 stakeholders and close in 60 to 90 days. You have two SDRs and four AEs.

**Step 1: Map the buyer journey.** Your buyers typically discover they have a workflow problem, research automation approaches, define requirements, evaluate vendors, and validate with procurement. Map each phase to a sales action.

**Step 2: Define your stages.** Based on your deal complexity, you choose six stages: Prospect, Qualify, Discover, Demo, Propose, Close. You merge Present and Propose because your sales cycle is short enough that demos and proposals happen in the same motion.

**Step 3: Write exit criteria.** For Qualify, the exit criteria are: documented economic buyer, confirmed pain point, and a rough timeline. For Discover: the rep can articulate the prospect's primary pain, the business impact, the decision-making process, and the key stakeholders without looking at their notes [2].

**Step 4: Assign roles.** SDRs own Prospect and Qualify. AEs own Discover, Demo, Propose, and Close. RevOps owns the CRM configuration and pipeline hygiene.

**Step 5: Build the CRM.** Configure pipeline stages, automate follow-up sequences, and create dashboards for visibility [1].

**Step 6: Review quarterly.** Use stage conversion data and rep feedback to refine each stage [2].

## Business case

Companies with a formalized sales process see up to 28% higher revenue growth than those without one [2]. Process-led sales teams outperform their peers by 25 to 30% on win rate [3]. Companies with a well-defined sales process are roughly 30% more likely to hit quota compared to those relying on individual rep intuition [3].

One mid-market SaaS company reduced its average sales cycle from 120 to 82 days by tightening qualification criteria and automating follow-up sequences [3]. They did not hire more reps or cut prices; they optimized what they already had [3].

> **The compounding effect:** every improvement to the process compounds over time. Tighten qualification and your pipeline gets cleaner. Cleaner pipeline and your forecasts get more accurate. Accurate forecasts and your hiring and capacity planning get better. This is the loop that turns a sales team from reactive to predictable.

## Agentic SOP

The agentic layer on this playbook:

- **Prospect stage:** An agent monitors intent data and surfaces accounts showing buying signals. It enriches contact data and drafts personalized outreach.
- **Qualify stage:** An agent cross-references call transcripts against BANT or MEDDIC criteria and flags gaps.
- **Discover stage:** An agent generates pre-call research briefs covering the company's strategic initiatives, recent news, and competitive context.
- **Close stage:** An agent prepares the handoff document for customer success, including pain points, success criteria, key stakeholders, and commitments made during negotiation.

A stage definition template for your CRM configuration:

```
Stage: Qualify
Owner: SDR
Entry criteria: Account identified, contact confirmed
Exit criteria: Documented economic buyer, confirmed pain point, rough timeline
Required activities:
  - BANT or MEDDIC assessment
  - Buying committee map
  - Pain point documentation
Automation:
  - Enrich contact data on stage entry
  - Notify AE on stage exit
```
