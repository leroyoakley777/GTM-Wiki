# ABM: Case Studies

ABM is the channel for a short list of high-value accounts, where a
single win pays for the whole motion. These cases prove the core
claim: ABM wins by *narrowing and personalizing*, not by scale. Spend
less on fewer, better-aimed accounts, and win rate and ROI follow.

Every number here carries a named source and an "as of" date.
Vendor-sourced results are flagged `(vendor source)`. Treat the
multiples as proof of what is achievable with intent, not a contract
on what any team will hit. The channel page, [ABM, the
channel](/docs/channels/abm), carries the benchmark context this
section proves possible. Start there for the where and when, then
return here for the proof.

---

## One Number That Matters Most

Account win rate on the target list. Most ABM reporting stops at
engagement: impressions, visits, touches. Those measure activity, not
outcome. The number that tells you the program works is whether you
win the accounts on the list at a higher rate than the rest of your
book.

Snowflake reported a 45% win rate on its ABM accounts versus 18% on
non-ABM accounts ([SalesMotion,
ABM Metrics](https://salesmotion.io/blog/account-based-marketing-metrics)).
DocuSign moved its enterprise win rate from 25% to 52% after adopting
ABM (same SalesMotion source). That gap, not engagement volume, is
the point.

---

## What ABM Is: The Harpoon, Not the Net

Demand gen casts a net and lets leads self-select. ABM names the
accounts that must be won, then treats each one as a market of its
own. It is a structural commitment to fewer, deeper, higher-value
relationships, not a tactic bolted onto a demand-gen machine.

Three execution tiers, matched to account value and probability
([Demandbase, ABM
101](https://www.demandbase.com/faq/account-based-marketing-101/)):

| Tier | Also called | Account count | Customization | Per-account effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| One-to-one | Strategic ABM | 1–50 | Fully custom, account plan per account | Very high |
| One-to-few | ABM Lite | 5–500, clustered | Semi-custom, per cluster of shared needs | Medium |
| One-to-many | Programmatic ABM | Hundreds–thousands | Templated, account-based ads + automation | Low |

One-to-one treats each account as a market of one. One-to-few groups
accounts that share a business issue or industry. One-to-many scales
personalization to hundreds of named accounts
([Demandbase](https://www.demandbase.com/faq/account-based-marketing-101/)).

**The tier trap:** every account drifting up-tier without a new plan,
or down-tier without a cheaper machine. Assign a tier at selection and
reassign only on evidence. The cases below mix tiers on purpose. They
do not run everything one-to-one.

## What Each Company Did

### Snowflake: the flagship

Scaled 1:1 ABM across 2,000+ top accounts with hyper-personalization,
then layered on an AI meeting-propensity model. The model targets the
accounts most likely to book, generates AI-tested ad copy, and spends
38% less while lifting booked meetings 2.3x
([Snowflake blog, Apr
2025](https://www.snowflake.com/en/blog/ai-driven-abm-b2b-growth/),
primary source, named staff, LinkedIn A/B results).

Hillary Carpio, Director of ABM at Snowflake, reported a 50% new
opportunity rate on existing customers targeted with ABM
([AdRoll/NextRoll
case](https://www.adroll.com/solutions/account-based-marketing),
`(vendor source)`). The account win-rate story, 45% vs 18%
([SalesMotion](https://salesmotion.io/blog/account-based-marketing-metrics)),
is the single most-cited ABM win-rate figure in the category.

This is the rare case with a primary source. The AI-ABM numbers come
from Snowflake's own blog with named staff and A/B test results. Where
a primary and a vendor source both exist, the primary wins.

### SAP Concur: one-to-many at enterprise scale

A large universe of accounts, a limited budget, and a demand to
prioritize. SAP Concur used Demandbase to consolidate account insights
from Salesforce, Marketo, and Adobe Analytics, layer intent signals,
and run personalized ads to cohorts
([Demandbase case,
2024](https://www.demandbase.com/resources/case-study/sap-concur-elevates-every-metric/),
`(vendor source)`).

Results, per Demandbase: 60% lift in web visits from advertised
accounts, engagement up from 51% to 95%, closed-won revenue up 52%,
deal size up 57%, pipeline up 59%, after three to four months of
consistent advertising to a prioritized list.

The mechanism is the transferable part: consolidate account
intelligence, rank the list with intent, segment into cohorts, run
coordinated ads, and share conversion alerts across sales and
marketing. That is one-to-many ABM executed cleanly at scale.

### BillingTree: bounded lists beat spray

Creative direct mail to just 100 accounts. Sixty percent response,
15% conversion, $350K in opportunities closed, 700% ROI
([CXL via
UviaUs](https://cxl.com)). No broad campaign. A short list, a strong
message, deep per-account effort.

### Invoca: omnichannel around one event

Pre-event omnichannel outreach ahead of Dreamforce aimed at priority
accounts: 35 new pipeline opportunities, 50+ meetings with priority
accounts, 33x ROI on campaign spend
([CXL via
PFL](https://cxl.com)). The event was the anchor. Outreach ran
across channels before it.

### Schneider Electric: one reporting picture

Unified ABM reporting across channels so sales and marketing saw the
same picture. Revenue from 21% of target accounts increased, and the
sales cycle sped up
([CXL](https://cxl.com)). No single glamorous multiple: the win is
alignment plus measurement.

### iRidium: the tiny-budget proof

A $3K-budget, ICP-narrowed virtual summit: 2,320 signups, 39% net-new
accounts, 34 SQLs, and five customers at $100K+ LTV in weeks, against
a 9-12 month sales cycle
([CXL via
Fullfunnel](https://cxl.com)). The budget was trivial. The narrow list
and the direct-to-ICP message did the work.

### DocuSign: enterprise win-rate lift

Enterprise win rate moved from 25% to 52% after adopting ABM
([SalesMotion](https://salesmotion.io/blog/account-based-marketing-metrics)).
This is the cleanest single-company before-and-after for the "win rate
is the outcome" claim.

## Pattern: A Harpoon Beats a Net

Every case targets a *bounded* account list and invests
disproportionate effort per account. BillingTree used 100 accounts.
Snowflake used an AI model to find the accounts most likely to book.
Invoca aimed at priority accounts before one event. iRidium ran a
$3K summit at an ICP-narrowed list. SAP Concur prioritized thousands
of accounts into cohorts and spent on the ones showing intent.

The recurring success factor is cross-team alignment: sales,
marketing, field, and SDR on one message aimed at one short list.
Demandbase's SAP Concur case names it explicitly: shared lists,
insights, and conversion alerts across marketing, field marketing,
MDRs, and sales.

The numbers prove the concentration logic. Less spend can beat a
broad campaign: 38% less spend (Snowflake), a $3K budget (iRidium),
100 accounts (BillingTree). Narrow, aligned, per-account beats wide
and generic.

## Mechanics: Target List to Intent to Outreach and Ads to Sales

Four steps, in order. Each case runs some version of this pipeline.

1. **Target account list.** From the [ICP](/docs/foundations/icp-definition)
   and [Channels Bullseye](/docs/foundations/gtm-channels-bullseye),
   build a named list of accounts with fit scores. Selection is the
   program. A mediocre list executed flawlessly beats a perfect motion
   aimed at the wrong accounts.
2. **Intent data.** Rank the list by buying signals. Snowflake built a
   meeting-propensity model. SAP Concur layered Demandbase intent on
   top of its firmographic list. Intent orders the list. Fit defines
   it.
3. **Personalized outreach and ads.** The coordinated multi-channel
   touch: ABM ads to named accounts, account-specific sequences,
   direct mail, field events. All channels reinforce one message.
4. **Sales orchestration.** When an account warms past a defined
   threshold, sales takes over with an account plan. The handoff is
   where ABM dies most often. Define the war-of-signal threshold in
   writing before you scale.

The direct-outreach component of step 3 lives in the [Outbound from
Zero playbook](/docs/playbooks/outbound-from-zero). The win-rate and
conversion benchmarks this pipeline feeds live in [GTM
Metrics](/docs/data/gtm-metrics).

## Math, Worked

Named bases, worked through. This is a model, not a measured result. The
point is to see how the pieces compound.

### Intent-prioritized conversion

Intent data justifies the ranking step. Intent-prioritized accounts
convert at 21.3% versus 8.4% for non-prioritized accounts, and the
median sales cycle compresses by 28 days
([2024 B2B Buying Study, via B2B Intent Data Benchmarks
2025](https://www.thestarrconspiracy.com/insights/benchmarks/b2b-intent-data-benchmarks-2025)).
Intent-based ads produce 220% higher click-through than control-group
campaigns ([Foundry](https://foundryco.com/blog/blog-top-30-account-based-marketing-and-intent-data-statistics-to-know/)).

Adoption follows: 71% of B2B marketers report active use of
third-party intent data in ABM, up from 55% in 2022 (industry ABM
benchmark survey, 2024, n=312, via the same B2B Intent Data
Benchmarks 2025 source).

**Worked example, named base.** Take a 1,000-account one-to-many
program at $25K ACV. Fit scoring holds 500. Intent data flags the top
100 as in-market.

- 100 intent-prioritized accounts x 21.3% = about 21 opportunities.
- 400 non-prioritized accounts x 8.4% = about 34 opportunities.
- Total around 55 opportunities from 500 accounts.

Without intent ranking, all 500 convert at 8.4% = 42 opportunities.
The ranking step adds roughly 13 opportunities before a single message
changes. At $25K ACV that is about $325K of extra pipeline from the
same list, attributable to ordering alone.

### Win-rate compound

Now layer the win rate. Snowflake's ABM win rate is 45%. Its non-ABM
baseline is 18% ([SalesMotion](https://salesmotion.io/blog/account-based-marketing-metrics)).

- Intent-prioritized: 21 x 45% about 9 closed-won.
- Non-prioritized: 34 x 18% about 6 closed-won.
- All-at-8.4% baseline: 42 x 18% about 8 closed-won.

The intent + ABM combination takes the same 500-account list from
about 8 to about 15 closed-won, roughly doubling revenue from the
list before you touch a message. The two effects, conversion ordering
and win-rate lift, multiply, not add.

### ROI check

Compare spend per account. One-to-many runs templated ads and email at
low cost per account. One-to-one runs a written account plan and an
exec touch per account. If the one-to-one accounts are 20x the ACV of
the one-to-many accounts, the higher per-account cost justified by
the per-account payoff. If not, you are paying one-to-one prices for
one-to-many economics: that is the tier trap.

### Account-list-size check

Most ABM orgs run a bounded list: 57% target 1,000 accounts or fewer
([Spiceworks](https://www.spiceworks.com/marketing/marketing-automation/articles/75-account-based-marketing-abm-statistics-every-marketer-should-know/)),
and 29% focus on 101-500
([Demand Gen Report, 2020 ABM
survey](https://www.demandgenreport.com/resources/reports/2020-abm-benchmark-survey-report-doubling-down-on-abm-research-shows-more-b2b-brands-stepping-up-their-investments-approaches-to-account-selection-segmentation-channel-strategies/)).
If your "ABM list" is 5,000 accounts, it is demand gen with an ABM
label.

## How It Varies by Segment

ABM is not one motion. The case matrix generalizes into two segment
shapes, per the [ABM channel page](/docs/channels/abm):

| Dimension | Enterprise ABM | Mid-Market ABM |
|---|---|---|
| Typical tier | One-to-one, some one-to-few | One-to-few, one-to-many |
| Account count | Tens | Hundreds |
| Sales cycle | 9-12 months | 1-4 months |
| Buying committee | 8-12 people, executive-led | 3-5 people, functional lead |
| Primary channels | Exec engagement, field, custom content, direct sales | Ads, email, events, SDR outreach |
| Key metric | Win rate, ACV, account penetration | Meetings, pipeline from target accounts |
| Example wins | Snowflake 45% vs 18%; 2.3x meetings, 38% less spend | iRidium $3K summit, 5 customers at $100K+ LTV |
| Economics | Justifies deep per-account investment | Justifies semi-customized, reusable plays |

SMB ABM runs on one-to-many economics: low ACV, high account count,
templated ads and email, and the win is measured in meetings and
pipeline from target accounts, not per-account penetration. Enterprise
ABM justifies a written account plan, exec-to-exec touches, and field
events because a single deal can be millions in ACV.

The mix matters more than the label. Most orgs run one-to-many for
breadth, one-to-few for mid-tier accounts, and one-to-one for the
handful that would change the quarter
([Demandbase](https://www.demandbase.com/faq/account-based-marketing-101/)).

## Failure Modes and Fixes

Every documented failure is a selection, alignment, measurement, or
scaling failure. The fixes are concrete.

- **ABM as vanity "marketing theater."** Activity reported as outcome:
  impressions, visits, touches. *Fix:* account win rate is the outcome
  metric. Engagement is a leading indicator, never the headline.
- **No sales alignment.** Marketing owns the list alone, sales never
  buys in, wins are not closed. *Fix:* joint ownership of the list and
  the account plan. Alignment is the mechanism, not a side effect:
  companies using account-based strategies see an 86% improvement in
  win rates when sales and marketing align
  ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/smarterwithgartner/3-pillars-of-a-successful-account-based-strategy)).
- **List too big.** Every account treated as tier-1, nobody treated as
  tier-1 well. *Fix:* enforce the tier split. A 5,000-account "ABM
  list" is demand gen with a label.
- **Intent data noise.** High-intent, low-fit accounts pursued because
  they are loud. *Fix:* fit defines the list, intent orders it. An
  account with low fit and high intent is a trap.
- **Scaling before proof.** One-to-many volume added before the message
  and list are proven on a small set. *Fix:* prove on a short list
  first, then scale the tiers that convert.
- **No written account plan.** Parallel touches called a strategy.
  *Fix:* the plan artifact is a gate, not a nice-to-have.
- **No handoff discipline.** Engaged accounts never reach a human with
  a plan. *Fix:* a war-of-signal threshold that triggers direct sales
  escalation.

The top reported ABM challenges confirm where programs break: proving
ROI (47%), aligning sales and marketing (43%), and scaling (40%)
([Demand Gen Report, 2025 ABM Benchmark
Survey](https://www.demandgenreport.com/resources/2025-account-based-marketing-benchmark-survey/50610/)).
None of the three is a tool problem. All three are operating problems.

## Measurement: Pipeline from Named Accounts, Not Raw Volume

Measure the outcome, not the activity. Four metrics carry the program:

1. **Account win rate.** Wins on the target list divided by target-list
   deals. Snowflake's 45% vs 18% and DocuSign's 25% to 52% are the
   anchors. Benchmark against your own non-ABM baseline in the same
   quarter, not an industry average.
2. **Pipeline attributed to named accounts.** Sum the pipeline from
   accounts on the list. An account that engages at 60% is closer than
   a lead that opens one email. See [GTM
   Metrics](/docs/data/gtm-metrics) for the pipeline-coverage context.
3. **Engagement lift.** Web visits, engagement rate, and penetration of
   the buying committee. SAP Concur's 51% to 95% engagement and 60% web
   visit lift are the shape of a working program. These are leading
   indicators, not outcomes.
4. **ABM vs non-ABM conversion.** The intent-prioritized conversion gap
   (21.3% vs 8.4%) and the win-rate gap (45% vs 18%) are the two
   numbers that separate a working program from theater.

Deal size compounds the story: ABM deals run roughly 20% larger than
traditional demand gen, and revenue attribution to ABM reaches up to
73% of total revenue in some orgs
([Huble, 111 ABM
Statistics](https://huble.com/blog/account-based-marketing-statistics)).
The win is not just more conversions. It is larger ones.

## Agentic Layer

ABM is a strong candidate for an agent use because the bottleneck
is the *thinking per account*: research, fit scoring, intent ranking,
and plan drafting. Agents do that at volume. Humans keep judgment on
selection and the send. The full treatment is [Agentic
Outbound](/docs/agentic/agentic-outbound) and the [Agentic
Stack](/docs/agentic/agentic-stack). The [guardrails](/docs/agentic/guardrails-and-measurement)
are non-negotiable.

**The rule that never changes:** the gate stays, the execution layer
changes. Humans define fit and approve the list. Agents remove the
research and ranking bottleneck.

| Motion step | Agent does | Human approves |
|---|---|---|
| List build | Sources accounts, scores fit, layers intent, ranks | Signs off on the final tier list |
| Account plan | Drafts the plan from a research brief: problem, committee, angle, value case | Approves the pattern, then the plans |
| Scoring / routing | Re-scores on new intent, flags accounts that crossed the ready threshold | Sets the threshold, reviews flagged accounts |
| Outreach drafting | Writes per-account reason-to-reach and outreach copy | Approves the message pattern |
| Reporting | Tracks account win rate and engagement against the list | Reads the debrief, adjusts selection |

### One-prompt starter for building a target account list and scoring intent

Adapt to your stack:

```text
You are running account-based marketing for {ICP} in {segment}, on a
target list from {data source}. For each account, return a row with:
fit score (0-100, weighted per the fit model), intent signal and its
source, tier (one-to-one / one-to-few / one-to-many), a named buyer
problem, and a one-line reason-to-reach grounded in a concrete signal.
Rank the list by fit and intent. Flag any account below the fit
threshold: those do not belong on the list. Then draft an account
plan for every tier-1 account: committee, angle, value case, channel
mix, and milestones. Return only accounts that pass the fit gate.
```

### One-prompt starter for drafting account-specific sequences

```text
For each tier-1 account on the approved list, draft a 4-touch,
multi-channel sequence using the account plan. Each touch must name a
concrete signal from the account (a job change, a funding event, a
tech-stack shift, a recent hire) and tie the product to that signal.
Use the approved message pattern. Do not invent a signal. If the
account has none, mark it "no reason-to-reach" and hold the account.
Return the sequence as a table: touch number, channel, subject or
CTA, the signal it references, and the owner (SDR, AE, or exec).
```

### Guardrails

1. **Human approval before any send.** Agents draft and rank. Humans
   approve the list and the message pattern. A weak pattern scaled by
   an agent dies faster, not slower.
2. **Fit is non-negotiable.** Agents must not pull in high-intent,
   low-fit accounts because they are loud. The fit gate is hard.
3. **Score the agent, not the activity.** Measure account win rate and
   pipeline from target accounts, not plans drafted or emails sent.
   See [Guardrails and Measurement](/docs/agentic/guardrails-and-measurement).

## Transferable Takeaway

Pick the accounts where the win matters most, invest per-account,
align sales and marketing on one message, and measure pipeline from
target accounts, not raw volume. ABM is a structural commitment to
fewer, deeper, higher-value relationships. If you cannot name the 50
or 200 accounts that would change the quarter, this is demand gen
with ABM flavor, not ABM.

## Honest Caveat

Most of these are vendor- and agency-sourced outcomes (CXL's case
library via UviaUs, PFL, and Fullfunnel, plus Demandbase's SAP Concur case),
self-reported and skewed to the impressive. The exceptions are
Snowflake's AI-ABM numbers, which are primary, Snowflake's own blog,
Apr 2025, named staff, LinkedIn A/B results, and the win-rate anchors
(SalesMotion's 45% vs 18% and DocuSign's 25% to 52%), which are
independent of the vendor selling the tools.

Company-specific conditions, a category-defining product at a
category-defining moment, matter. The tactics transfer. The exact
multiples may not. Where a number is vendor-sourced, the `(vendor
source)` flag tells you the reader of the number, not the outcome.
